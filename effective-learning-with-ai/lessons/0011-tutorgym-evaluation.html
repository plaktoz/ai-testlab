Evaluating AI Tutors with TutorGym

The Evaluation Problem

How do you know whether an AI tutoring system is actually good at tutoring? The most obvious answer — measure whether students get the right answers — turns out to be deeply misleading.

Consider a tutoring system evaluated on a math problem set. A system that simply tells the student the answer will score 100% on final-answer accuracy. By that metric it looks perfect. But it has done nothing to build understanding, scaffold reasoning, or help the student develop the skill independently. It is, in any meaningful sense, a terrible tutor.

This is the core evaluation problem: final-answer benchmarks cannot distinguish between a system that teaches and a system that answers. Tutoring quality is inherently interactive and sequential — it lives in the moment-by-moment decisions a tutor makes as a student works through a problem, not in whether the final answer is correct.

To evaluate tutoring properly you need to observe what the AI does at each step: does it give a hint that nudges without revealing? Does it correctly identify when a student action is wrong? Does it pace feedback in a way that promotes learning rather than dependency? None of this shows up in a final-answer score.

TutorGym's Approach: Evaluation Inside Real ITS Domains

TutorGym solves the evaluation problem by embedding AI agents directly inside 223 existing Intelligent Tutoring System (ITS) domains — including Cognitive Tutors, Apprentice Tutors, and OATutors. The domains span subjects across mathematics, science, and skills-based learning.

Rather than asking "did the AI get the right final answer?", TutorGym asks "did the AI make the right tutoring move at each step?" The agent must operate within the ITS environment in real time: providing hints when prompted, labeling student actions as correct or incorrect, and deciding when and how to give feedback. Each of these moves is scored against the behavior the ITS expects from a competent tutor.

Because the 223 ITS domains already contain structured problem spaces, scripted student models, and well-defined correct tutoring behaviors, TutorGym can run fully automated evaluations without recruiting human students or running longitudinal studies. The evaluation infrastructure already exists — TutorGym just provides the harness to run AI agents through it.

Two Evaluation Modes

TutorGym supports two distinct evaluation modes depending on which role the AI occupies: tutor or student. The two modes probe completely different capabilities and reveal different failure patterns.

Mode What the AI does What is measured Key finding
Tutor mode Acts as the tutor; a scripted student works through the ITS problem Hint quality, correctness of feedback labels, appropriateness of step sequencing LLMs performed poorly — none did better than chance at labeling incorrect student actions; hint generation is the hardest unsolved problem
Student mode Acts as the student; a real ITS tutors it through the problem Whether the AI produces human-like learning curves — incremental improvement, characteristic errors, realistic plateau behavior LLMs produced remarkably human-like learning curves, closely matching the pattern of real student data

The two modes are complementary. Tutor mode reveals whether the AI can produce high-quality instructional moves. Student mode reveals whether the AI behaves like a realistic learner — a property that turns out to have significant implications for how the system can be used (see below).

Key Findings

As tutors: LLMs struggled

Sobering Result

When acting as tutors, none of the tested LLMs did better than chance at labeling incorrect student actions. Identifying what a student did wrong — and why — proved far harder than generating fluent text responses. Hint generation was identified as the hardest unsolved problem: producing a hint that guides without revealing requires precise knowledge of what the student knows, what the correct approach is, and exactly how much information to withhold. Current LLMs consistently over-hinted or mislabeled errors.

This finding matters for builders. An LLM that can explain a concept clearly in a standalone conversation may still fail badly when embedded in a structured tutoring loop where it must make precise, constrained moves at each step. Conversational fluency and step-level tutoring competence are not the same thing.

As students: LLMs were surprisingly realistic

The student-mode results were strikingly different. LLMs produced learning curves that closely resembled those of real human students — showing incremental improvement over sessions, making the kinds of errors that human learners make, and exhibiting realistic plateau behavior when concepts were difficult. This was not expected given how different LLM processing is from human cognition.

Key Implication

Because LLMs produce human-like learning curves in student mode, they can serve as synthetic students for evaluating and stress-testing tutoring systems — without needing to recruit and run studies with real learners. This insight directly enables the synthetic student simulation approach covered in Lesson 13. It is one of the most practically significant findings in recent ITS research.

Practical Use for Builders

For anyone building a tutoring system, TutorGym provides a principled evaluation path that avoids the two most common traps:

  • Avoid final-answer benchmarks. They reward systems that answer rather than teach. A system that gives hints and guides gets penalized compared to a system that just states the answer — the opposite of what you want.
  • Avoid waiting for longitudinal human studies. Collecting real student learning-curve data requires months, institutional access, and significant ethical overhead. TutorGym's ITS domains let you run equivalent evaluations immediately against scripted student models.

The most actionable entry point for a new builder is student mode. Run your system inside one of TutorGym's ITS domains and check whether it produces realistic learning curves. If it does, you have evidence that its behavior is human-like in a meaningful sense. If it doesn't — if it flatlines immediately or shows implausible step-level behavior — that is a signal worth investigating before any human study.

Tutor mode is the harder bar. Use it to surface specific failure patterns in your hint generation and feedback labeling logic. The finding that LLMs struggle at step-level labeling suggests this is where careful prompt engineering, retrieval augmentation, or specialized fine-tuning will be most valuable.

Skill Exercise

Design a minimal TutorGym-style evaluation for a tutoring system you might build. Specify:

  1. The domain your tutoring system covers.
  2. What a "good tutoring move" looks like in that domain — be concrete about what the tutor should do at a specific student step.
  3. One way the system could score 100% on a naive metric while still being a bad tutor.
