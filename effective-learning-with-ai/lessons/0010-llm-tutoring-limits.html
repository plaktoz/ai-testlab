What Off-the-Shelf LLMs Can't Do (Yet)

Module 4 — Evaluation & State of the Art Lesson 10 of 13
Primary Source
Can Large Language Models Match Tutoring System Adaptivity? A Benchmarking Study arxiv.org/abs/2504.05570 — tests Llama3-8B, Llama3-70B, and GPT-4o across 75 real-world ITS scenarios (1,350 instructional moves)

The Benchmark

The study constructs a rigorous evaluation from 75 real-world scenarios drawn from proven adaptive tutoring systems — the kind of Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) that have been deployed in classrooms and studied for decades. These scenarios yield 1,350 discrete instructional moves: specific decision points where the tutoring system must choose what to say or ask next, given where the student is in their understanding.

For each scenario, the benchmark records what a real ITS would do — the adaptive decision a purpose-built system makes. The question is whether an LLM, prompted to act as a tutor, makes the same decision. The test is not whether the LLM gives a correct factual answer; it is whether the LLM exhibits the same instructional behavior as a system explicitly designed to adapt to learners.

Headline Finding

Even GPT-4o — the strongest model tested — "only marginally mimics the adaptivity of ITS." Across all three models studied, the best performance fell far short of what purpose-built tutoring systems do routinely. Off-the-shelf LLMs are not a drop-in replacement for adaptive tutoring infrastructure.

Three Specific Failure Modes

The benchmark identifies three recurring ways LLMs diverge from how real ITS systems behave. These are not edge cases — they are structural differences in how the models approach instructional interaction.

1. Overly Direct Feedback

GPT-4o tends to give the answer too quickly. Real ITS systems operate on a different principle: they withhold the answer and instead provide hints that progressively narrow the student's search space. The idea is to keep the student actively reasoning — making the cognitive work visible — rather than short-circuiting it with a complete solution. LLMs default to "here's the answer," which eliminates exactly the effortful retrieval practice that produces durable learning.

2. Failure to Use Open-Ended Questioning

Real ITS systems regularly ask questions like "what do you think?" or "why might that be?" — moves that force the student to construct an explanation rather than receive one. The benchmark finds that LLMs rarely make these moves. They prefer declarative, complete responses: the model explains, the student reads. This pattern is comfortable and fluent, but it transfers the cognitive load away from the learner, which is the opposite of what effective tutoring does.

3. No Explicit Student Knowledge Modeling

Perhaps the most structurally significant gap: LLMs have no persistent model of the individual student's knowledge state. Each API call begins from scratch, or from whatever context the developer has manually assembled and injected into the prompt. Real ITS systems, by contrast, maintain continuous probabilistic models — Bayesian Knowledge Tracing or equivalent — that update with every student interaction and inform every subsequent instructional decision. An LLM has no such model unless one is built and supplied externally.

What GPT-4o Does Well

The picture is not entirely negative. The benchmark surfaces a clear strength: GPT-4o follows instructions reliably. If you write a detailed system prompt that specifies the student's current knowledge state, the rules for hint sequencing, and explicit guidelines for when to ask versus tell, GPT-4o can execute those rules consistently.

The gap is not in the model's ability to follow a student model — it is in the model's ability to automatically build and maintain one. Supply the state; GPT-4o can act on it. Expect the model to construct the state on its own across sessions; it cannot.

LLMs vs. ITS Systems: What the Benchmark Reveals

What LLMs Do Well What ITS Systems Do That LLMs Don't
Follow explicit tutoring rules in a detailed system prompt Automatically build and update a student knowledge model
Generate fluent, factually accurate explanations on demand Withhold answers and provide calibrated progressive hints
Adapt tone and vocabulary to a stated learner level Proactively ask open-ended questions to force student cognition
Handle diverse subject domains without domain-specific retraining Maintain persistent per-student state across multiple sessions
Produce coherent multi-turn dialogue when given prior context Make adaptive decisions from a continuous Bayesian knowledge trace

The Design Implication

The benchmark's findings converge on a single practical conclusion about system architecture. An off-the-shelf LLM wrapped in a prompt is not itself a tutoring system. It is a tutoring system interface. It can deliver instructions, generate explanations, and apply rules — but it cannot supply the adaptive machinery on its own.

Key Distinction

LLM = interface; system = the infrastructure around it. The student knowledge model, the hint sequencing logic, and the open-ended questioning rules must be built and maintained externally — then passed into the LLM as structured context. The model executes; the system decides.

This is a useful reframe for anyone designing AI-powered learning tools. The question is not "can GPT-4o tutor students?" — it demonstrably can, in a limited sense. The question is "what infrastructure needs to exist around it to make it adaptive?" The benchmark gives a clear answer: at minimum, you need persistent student knowledge modeling, explicit hint sequencing, and rules for when to prompt student reasoning rather than supply an answer.
