Constitutional AI — The Adversarial Self-Critique Architecture

Estimated reading time: ~30 minutes Module: Multi-Agent Architectures
Primary Source: Bai, Y. et al., Anthropic (2022). Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback. arXiv:2212.08073. Key result: A two-stage pipeline using AI-generated critiques and preference labels achieves harmlessness comparable to human-feedback RLHF while requiring far fewer human annotations.

Overview and Motivation

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) became the standard approach to aligning large language models in the early 2020s, but it carries a hard practical constraint: every preference judgment requires a human annotator to read two model responses and choose the safer or more helpful one. At the scale needed to cover millions of diverse prompts, that annotation cost is prohibitive.

Constitutional AI, introduced by Bai et al. at Anthropic in 2022, proposes a different answer: give the model a written constitution — a list of principles in natural language — and let the model evaluate its own outputs against those principles without human involvement at the labeling step. The constitution replaces the human annotator as the arbiter of what counts as harmful, enabling a fully automated critique-revision-retraining cycle.

The method is a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1, called Supervised Learning from AI Feedback (SL-CAI), uses the model's own critiques to generate cleaner training data. Stage 2, called RLHF from AI Feedback (RLAIF), replaces human preference labels with AI-generated labels to train a reward model for reinforcement learning. Together the two stages progressively drive the model toward outputs that satisfy every principle in the constitution, producing a model that is both harmless and transparent about the reasons behind its refusals.

Stage 1 — The Critique-Revision Loop (SL-CAI)

Stage 1 is built around a five-step loop that the model runs on each red-team prompt before any fine-tuning occurs.

Step 1 — Elicit a harmful response. A red-team prompt is used to coax the model into producing output that violates at least one constitutional principle. This is intentionally adversarial: the goal is to get the model to say something it should not say, so that the subsequent critique step has something real to work with.

Step 2 — Critique the response. The model is then shown its own harmful output and asked to identify specific ways in which that response is harmful, unethical, or biased — evaluated against a specific constitutional principle. An example critique prompt from the paper reads: "Identify specific ways in which the assistant's response is harmful, unethical, racist, or socially biased."

Step 3 — Revise the response. The model is asked to rewrite its earlier response so that the identified violation is removed. The revision is not a simple refusal; the model is expected to provide a genuinely more helpful answer that still satisfies the user's underlying intent, wherever that is possible without causing harm.

Steps 4 and 5 — Repeat and fine-tune. The critique-revision loop is executed up to N times per prompt, applying successive constitutional principles in sequence. After all iterations, the model is fine-tuned on the final revised responses. Because those responses were generated by the model itself (prompted to apply the constitution), they form a training set that is cleaner than raw human-written demonstrations yet requires no human labeling of individual examples.

Stage 2 — RLHF from AI Feedback (RLAIF)

After Stage 1 produces an SL-CAI model, Stage 2 applies reinforcement learning using preference labels that the SL-CAI model generates — a technique the paper calls RLAIF.

Step 1 — Generate response pairs. For each prompt, the SL-CAI model generates two candidate responses. These two responses differ in how well they satisfy the constitutional principles, creating a natural comparison pair.

Step 2 — AI preference labeling. The SL-CAI model is then asked, in natural language, which of the two responses better follows the constitution. This produces a binary preference label for each pair — the same format as a human preference label in standard RLHF, but generated by the model at a fraction of the cost.

Step 3 — Train a reward model. A preference model (also called a reward model) is trained on the AI-generated labels. This model learns to score any given response by how well it satisfies the constitution, without needing to re-query the LLM at inference time.

Step 4 — Apply PPO. The SL-CAI model is fine-tuned further using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), with the preference model providing the reward signal. This stage drives the model to produce responses that reliably score well under the reward model — and therefore reliably satisfy the constitutional principles — even on prompts it has not seen before.

The final result of both stages is a model that achieves harmlessness scores comparable to human-labeled RLHF, while also becoming more transparent: rather than producing terse refusals, the model explicitly explains why it will not comply with a harmful request, pointing toward the relevant constitutional principle.

The Constitution: Principles in Natural Language

The "constitution" is not a formal logic system or a programmatic filter. It is a list of principles written in ordinary natural language, which the model reads and reasons about in the same way it reasons about any text. This is both a strength and a design choice: natural language principles are easy to update, easy to explain to external auditors, and flexible enough to cover novel situations that a hand-coded rule set would miss.

Anthropic's constitution draws from multiple sources. Several principles are excerpted directly from the United Nations Universal Declaration of Human Rights, covering broad protections around dignity, equality, and non-discrimination. Others are more operationally specific safety guidelines targeting known failure modes in language model outputs — for example, principles that address the generation of instructions for physical harm, the promotion of illegal activity, or the production of content that demeans particular groups.

During the critique-revision loop, principles are applied in sequence. Each iteration instructs the model to evaluate the current response against one specific principle and revise accordingly. The sequential application means that later iterations can catch harms missed by earlier ones, and that no single principle carries the full burden of safety. A response must survive all principles in the constitution to be accepted as a clean training example.

This design allows practitioners to tune the model's behavior by editing the constitution — adding principles to address new harm categories, removing principles that prove too restrictive, or reweighting by controlling which principles appear earliest in the sequence. The constitution is therefore a legible, auditable interface between human values and model behavior.

Constitutional AI as a Collapsed Attacker-Defender System

From the perspective of adversarial agent design, Constitutional AI has a striking structural property: it collapses what would normally be a two-agent attacker-defender system into the sequential processing of a single model. In Stage 1, the same model first generates a harmful response (acting as attacker) and then critiques and corrects it (acting as defender). No second model is needed.

This is made possible by the fact that language models can switch roles within a conversation by following different prompts. The red-team prompt positions the model as a compliant, unconstrained responder. The critique prompt re-positions it as a constitutional evaluator. The revision prompt re-positions it as a careful editor. Each role shift is a prompt engineering move, not an architectural one.

The critique step serves a specific function that is easy to underestimate: it prevents sycophancy. Left to its own devices, a model prompted to "improve" its previous response may simply rewrite it in more polished language while preserving the underlying harm. Forcing a critique step — asking the model to explicitly identify what is wrong before asking it to revise — breaks that pattern. The model must acknowledge the violation before it can correct it, which makes cosmetic rewrites much less likely.

The RLAIF stage in Stage 2 extends this insight to preference learning. Rather than collapsing attacker and defender into one sequential pipeline, it uses the model itself as the preference annotator — a role normally assigned to human raters. The model is presented with two of its own responses and asked to judge which one better satisfies the constitution. This turns the model into its own evaluator, closing the feedback loop entirely within the AI system while retaining human authorship at the level of the constitution.

Key Insight: Constitutional AI is an attacker-defender multi-agent system compressed into one model's sequential context window. The same model generates the harmful output, critiques it, and revises it — playing attacker and defender in turn, governed by a human-authored constitution that acts as the referee. This architecture scales alignment supervision without scaling human annotation.
Common Misconception: Constitutional AI does not eliminate human oversight — it relocates it. The constitution itself must still be written and curated by humans, and its quality directly determines the model's behavior. A poorly written or incomplete constitution can leave entire harm categories unaddressed. The method reduces the need for human preference labels at training time, but it amplifies the importance of getting the constitution right: errors in the principles propagate through every training example generated by the critique-revision loop.
