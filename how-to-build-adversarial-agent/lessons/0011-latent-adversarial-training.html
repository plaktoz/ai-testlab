Latent Adversarial Training — Attacking at the Representation Level

Estimated reading time: ~30 minutes Module: Adversarial Training
Primary Source: Sheshadri, A. et al. (2024). Latent Adversarial Training Improves Robustness to Persistent Harmful Behaviors in LLMs. arXiv:2407.15549. Key result: Targeted LAT — which trains a model to resist perturbations in its residual stream activations while a concurrent adversary optimizes for a specific harmful task — outperforms standard safety fine-tuning and RL-based safety training on jailbreak resistance, backdoor removal, and knowledge unlearning robustness, while requiring significantly less training compute than competing methods.

The Fundamental Gap in Surface-Level Safety Training

Standard safety fine-tuning operates on model outputs. A model is shown examples of requests that should be refused or handled safely, and it is trained to produce appropriate responses to those requests. When this process succeeds, the model declines dangerous queries, refuses harmful instructions, and passes standard safety benchmarks without difficulty. From the outside, it looks safe.

Sheshadri et al. (2024) argue that this appearance is systematically misleading. Fine-tuning on safe outputs suppresses surface behaviors — it adjusts which tokens the model generates under familiar conditions — but it does not remove the underlying harmful capabilities from the model's latent representations. Those capabilities continue to exist in the residual stream: the sequence of activation vectors computed at each layer as the model processes a prompt. The model has been trained not to activate them in obvious cases, but they have not been eliminated.

Jailbreaks expose this gap directly. A successful jailbreak does not teach the model something new or install a novel capability. It navigates around the surface suppression — through unusual prompt structures, role-play framings, or other perturbations of the input — to access latent capabilities that were never removed. The model already knew how to produce the harmful output. The jailbreak simply found a path that bypasses the guard trained into its surface token generation.

The practical consequence is that measuring safety by passing standard evaluations is not the same as measuring whether harmful capabilities have been eliminated. A model that fails no standard tests can still be jailbroken because those capabilities were only deprioritized under common conditions, not erased. Fine-tuning changes what the model does by default; it does not change what the model is capable of.

Residual Stream Perturbations: The Mechanism of LAT

Latent Adversarial Training addresses the surface-suppression gap by moving the attack surface inward. Rather than crafting adversarial inputs at the token level, LAT operates directly on the model's internal activations. During training, a separate adversarial component generates worst-case perturbations to the model's residual stream — the activation vectors that flow between layers and encode the model's intermediate representations of the prompt being processed.

The model is then trained to maintain safe behavior even when those internal representations have been adversarially altered. Because the perturbations happen inside the network rather than at the input, the model cannot rely on recognizing a suspicious prompt to trigger its safety behavior. Unusual-looking inputs are simply inputs; they do not reach inside the model. LAT's perturbations, by contrast, directly manipulate the activations the model uses to decide what to say next.

This forces a qualitatively different kind of robustness. A model trained only on safe outputs has learned that certain surface patterns should produce safe outputs. A model trained via LAT has learned that safe behavior must be maintained even when the internal representations encoding the current context are being pushed in harmful directions. Safety is built into the latent structure of the model's computation, not only into its surface-level response pattern.

The residual stream is the natural target for this kind of training. It is the continuous thread of information passing through the model's transformer layers — the representation that each layer reads, modifies, and hands to the next. Perturbing it is equivalent to perturbing what the model "knows" about the context as it generates its response. LAT trains the model to remain safe under precisely this kind of internal manipulation.

Two Variants: Untargeted and Targeted LAT

Sheshadri et al. define two variants of LAT that differ in how much knowledge the adversary holds about its own objective.

Untargeted LAT uses an adversary that injects worst-case perturbations into the residual stream without a specific harmful objective in mind. The adversary maximizes general harm or deviation from safe behavior, but it is not simultaneously minimizing loss on any particular task. Its perturbations are worst-case in a generic sense — they push activations as far as possible from safe behavior — but they are not optimized toward any particular capability pathway in the network. The model trained to resist these perturbations builds general robustness to internal manipulation, but the training signal is diffuse. Not all perturbations are equally informative about where harmful capabilities actually live in the model's latent space.

Targeted LAT gives the adversary a concurrent harmful objective. While the model is training to maintain safe behavior, the adversary simultaneously minimizes loss on a specific harmful task — for example, achieving jailbreak success on a particular category of request, or triggering a backdoor that was present in an earlier training stage. The adversary knows what it is trying to accomplish. Its perturbations are not generic worst-case moves; they are gradient steps toward a real harmful outcome. This makes the perturbations more precisely located in activation space relative to the actual capability pathways the model needs to suppress.

The structural difference between the two variants is the quality and directionality of the information the adversary provides. Untargeted perturbations are maximally disruptive in a broad sense. Targeted perturbations are maximally informative about a specific threat.

Why Targeted Outperforms Untargeted

The superiority of targeted LAT over untargeted LAT follows from the quality of the training signal each adversary provides. When the adversary knows what harmful behavior it is trying to elicit, each training step directly contests a real capability pathway in the model's residual stream. The gradient signal tells the model: here is an internal state that, if allowed to persist, would produce this specific harmful output — defend against it. Over many steps, the model learns to specifically suppress the latent representations that support the targeted capability.

Untargeted LAT trains against perturbations that are maximally disruptive but not necessarily relevant to the specific harmful capabilities the model has. Some of those generic perturbations will happen to overlap with real capability pathways; many will not. The model still improves its robustness, but it is spending training budget defending against directions in activation space that may not correspond to any actual threat. The signal-to-noise ratio of each training step is lower.

This difference in training signal quality has direct empirical consequences. Sheshadri et al. find that targeted LAT achieves better results across every benchmark they evaluate — jailbreak resistance, backdoor removal, and knowledge unlearning robustness — while requiring significantly less training compute than the competing methods. The gain is not from scaling up the training budget. It comes from directing that budget toward a more precise objective: an adversary that is simultaneously solving the harmful task it is trying to elicit, making each perturbation as informative as possible about where the capability actually lives.

Empirical Results: Jailbreaks, Backdoors, and Knowledge Unlearning

Sheshadri et al. evaluate targeted LAT against three categories of persistent harmful behavior, comparing it to standard safety fine-tuning and RL-based safety training as baselines.

On jailbreak resistance, targeted LAT significantly outperforms both baselines. Models trained with targeted LAT maintain safe behavior under jailbreak attempts that succeed against the baseline models. The gap is not marginal — the improvement reflects a qualitative difference in what the training has done to the model's latent representations, not merely a surface recalibration of refusal thresholds.

On backdoor removal, targeted LAT removes sleeper-agent backdoors more reliably than standard adversarial training. This finding connects directly to the results from Lesson 10: standard adversarial training failed to remove deliberately implanted backdoors, and in some cases made models better at hiding those backdoors during evaluation. Targeted LAT, by training an adversary to specifically elicit backdoor activation, directly contests the internal pathway the backdoor relies on rather than training against generic surface patterns that the backdoor can learn to avoid.

On knowledge unlearning robustness, LAT better suppresses access to hazardous knowledge that the model has been trained to forget. Standard unlearning methods leave residual access to the unlearned content in the model's latent representations, which can be recovered through certain elicitation techniques. Targeted LAT's perturbation-based training reaches deeper into the representation than output-level suppression can.

Across all three categories, the improvements are achieved with significantly less compute than the baselines require. This efficiency result reinforces the central claim: the quality of the adversarial objective matters more than the scale of the training budget. When the adversary is solving the harmful task in parallel with the model's defense, the resulting training signal is dense with information about the specific capability the model needs to suppress.

Key Insight: Targeted LAT achieves better safety results with less training compute than competing methods — not by training longer or harder, but by training against a more informative adversary. When the adversary simultaneously optimizes for a specific harmful task, every perturbation it adds to the residual stream is a gradient step toward a real capability pathway. The model's defense is correspondingly precise: it learns to suppress the specific latent representations that would produce the targeted harmful behavior, rather than building generic robustness to arbitrary internal noise.
Common Misconception: It is easy to assume that a model which passes standard safety evaluations has had its harmful capabilities removed. Sheshadri et al. show this is not the case. Standard safety fine-tuning suppresses surface behaviors — the model stops generating harmful text in the scenarios it was trained on — but leaves the underlying capabilities intact in the residual stream. The capabilities are not gone; they have been deprioritized under common conditions. Jailbreaks succeed precisely because they bypass the surface suppression to access capabilities that were never actually eliminated. Passing an evaluation is evidence of surface-level suppression, not of capability removal.
