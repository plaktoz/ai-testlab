Gradient-Based Attacks — Adversarial Suffixes That Transfer Across Models

~30 minutes Module: Attack Techniques
Primary Source: Zou et al. (2023), "Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks on Aligned Language Models," arXiv:2307.15043 — This paper introduces the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) method, demonstrating that adversarial suffixes optimized on open-source white-box models transfer to closed black-box commercial systems including ChatGPT, GPT-4, Bard, and Claude without any gradient access to those systems.

The Problem: Automating Adversarial Prompt Discovery

Prior to Zou et al. 2023, the dominant approach to jailbreaking large language models relied on human creativity: researchers and adversaries manually crafted prompts, role-play scenarios, and indirect phrasings designed to coax aligned models into producing harmful outputs. While effective in individual cases, this approach does not scale. Each new model variant, each new safety patch, and each new refusal style requires a fresh round of manual experimentation, making systematic evaluation of model robustness extremely difficult.

Zou et al. posed a sharper question: can adversarial prompts be discovered automatically, using the same gradient-based optimization techniques that underlie neural network training itself? The answer, documented in their paper, is yes. The key insight is that an aligned language model's resistance to harmful requests is ultimately encoded in its weights — and those weights are differentiable. If you can compute gradients, you can optimize.

The result is the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) algorithm: a fully automated method for discovering adversarial suffixes that, when appended to a harmful request, reliably cause a model to begin its response affirmatively rather than with a refusal. GCG requires no human intuition about what a jailbreak might look like. It treats adversarial prompt discovery as a discrete optimization problem and searches through token space guided entirely by gradient signals from the target model's weights.

The GCG Algorithm: Greedy Coordinate Gradient in Detail

The mechanics of GCG are precise and reproducible. Given a harmful request — for example, a query asking for instructions on a dangerous activity — GCG appends a short suffix: a sequence of tokens that initially carries no semantic meaning. The optimization target is to minimize the model's loss on a desired harmful completion. In other words, the algorithm searches for a suffix that makes the model assign high probability to producing a harmful response.

At each iteration, GCG computes the gradient of the target loss with respect to the one-hot encoding of each token in the suffix. This gradient signal identifies which token replacements at each position would most reduce the loss — that is, which tokens would make the model more likely to produce the harmful target output. The algorithm then evaluates a batch of candidate token replacements and selects the single swap that yields the greatest improvement across the suffix positions.

This is a greedy coordinate descent approach over discrete token space. Because token embeddings are discrete, GCG uses the gradient as an approximation to rank candidates, then validates the best candidate through a forward pass. The process repeats across many iterations, gradually shaping the suffix into a token sequence that reliably triggers the desired output. White-box access to the model — specifically access to its weights and activations for gradient computation — is required during optimization. Zou et al. used Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B as their white-box target models for the optimization phase.

White-Box Results: What GCG Achieves on Target Models

On the white-box models used during optimization, GCG achieves high attack success rates across a broad range of harmful requests. The suffixes it produces are not human-readable — they appear as nonsense sequences of tokens, often including unusual punctuation, fragments of unrelated words, or encoding artifacts. This unintelligibility is a by-product of pure loss minimization: GCG optimizes for the gradient objective, not for linguistic coherence or human interpretability.

A particularly important result is that a single suffix can be optimized to work across multiple harmful prompts simultaneously. Zou et al. demonstrate "universal" adversarial suffixes: a single token string that causes the model to comply with a wide variety of different harmful inputs, not just the specific request it was optimized against. This universality means an adversary does not need to run a fresh optimization for each new query — one suffix can serve as a general-purpose jailbreak for an entire category of harmful requests.

The white-box results establish a foundational baseline: on the models where GCG has direct gradient access, it fully automates adversarial suffix discovery without any human creativity. This is the first systematic demonstration that safety-aligned LLMs can be compromised through pure gradient-based optimization, directly analogous to the adversarial examples literature in computer vision but applied to discrete token sequences in language models.

Black-Box Transferability: Attacking Models You Cannot Access

The most consequential finding in the Zou et al. paper is that suffixes optimized on open-source white-box models transfer to commercial black-box models — including ChatGPT, GPT-4, Bard, and Claude — without any gradient access to those systems. This is a transfer attack: the adversary optimizes on one model and deploys the resulting suffix against a different, inaccessible model through its public API.

Transfer attacks are well-documented in the adversarial examples literature for image classifiers, but their effectiveness against large language models with safety fine-tuning was not established prior to this work. Zou et al. demonstrate that suffixes which drive Vicuna toward harmful completions also cause commercial models to drop their safety-trained refusals and begin complying with the original harmful request.

Both white-box and black-box settings are validated in the paper. In the white-box setting, GCG is run directly against the target model. In the black-box setting, suffixes are first optimized on Vicuna and then evaluated against commercial APIs with no further modification. Transfer success rates are lower than white-box results, as expected, but remain substantial enough to constitute a real security concern. This finding implies that safety-aligning a closed commercial model does not provide immunity against adversaries who have access to open-source models in the same general family.

Why Transferability Works: Shared Internal Representations

Transferability is not an accident — it reflects something structural about how large language models are trained. Safety alignment training uses similar data, similar techniques (RLHF, DPO, and related methods), and similar objectives across models from different organizations. As a result, safety-aligned models tend to develop similar internal representations of safety-relevant concepts, even when specific architectures and proprietary details differ significantly between them.

Zou et al. propose that a suffix optimized on one model occupies a similar geometric region in other models' embedding spaces, because alignment training on similar data creates similar internal structures. When GCG finds a suffix that pushes Vicuna toward a harmful completion, that suffix is navigating a region of the embedding space that corresponds to low safety inhibition — and other safety-aligned models share a geometrically similar structure in that same region, making the transfer attack effective.

This is a deep point about the nature of safety alignment as it is currently practiced. It suggests that surface-level differences between models — different parameter counts, different architectures, different proprietary training details — do not fully insulate them from transfer attacks, because the safety-relevant geometry emerges from shared training objectives and shared data distributions. An adversarial suffix is not merely a syntactic trick; it is a structured input that exploits the shared geometric consequences of similar alignment procedures across a family of models.

Key Insight: GCG is the first fully automated, gradient-based method for adversarial suffix optimization against aligned LLMs — and its most striking result is not the white-box attack itself, but the demonstration that suffixes transfer to black-box commercial models. Safety alignment on a closed system does not provide immunity against adversaries with gradient access to any open-source model in the same general family, because alignment training on similar data creates similar internal safety geometry across models.
Common Misconception: It is tempting to assume that the unintelligibility of GCG suffixes makes them easy to filter — a simple perplexity check or token-anomaly detector could flag the nonsense sequences before they reach the model. In practice, this surface-level defense is incomplete. Adversaries can incorporate fluency or perplexity constraints directly into the GCG optimization objective to produce more natural-looking suffixes, and the underlying vulnerability — the exploitable safety geometry shared across aligned models — is not addressed by input filtering alone. Filtering is a partial mitigation, not a structural solution.
