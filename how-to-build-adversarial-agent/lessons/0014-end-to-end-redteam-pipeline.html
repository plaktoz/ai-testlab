Designing an End-to-End Red-Teaming Pipeline

Estimated reading time: ~35 minutes Module: Practical Implementation

Why a Pipeline, Not a Point-in-Time Test

The most important insight driving this lesson comes from JailbreakRadar: evaluating attacks and defenses in isolation produces dangerously misleading results. Prior research had studied attack techniques in one paper and defensive measures in another, treating them as independent research questions. JailbreakRadar revealed that this methodology misses the critical interaction between the two entirely. An attack that achieves a 90% success rate against an undefended model might drop to 10% when a simple defense is applied. Siloed research would report a 90% attack success rate and a working defense — but never tell you that those two facts are connected and that together they make the attack largely irrelevant in practice.

This is not a subtle methodological nuance. It means that a team relying on isolated benchmark results might believe their model faces a severe threat from a particular attack technique, invest engineering effort in a complex countermeasure, and never learn that the existing, simpler defensive measure already handled the problem adequately. Conversely, a team that only measures defenses might declare their model protected without ever testing whether the defenses actually degrade attack success rates against the specific attack techniques deployed in the wild.

The pipeline framing is the solution. A pipeline forces you to stage attack generation, model testing, defense application, and outcome measurement in deliberate sequence, feeding results from each phase into the next. Building that sequence is not a bureaucratic formality — it is the minimum structure required to produce valid, actionable findings about the real-world vulnerability of a deployed system.

The JailbreakRadar Benchmark Architecture

JailbreakRadar provides a concrete reference architecture that pipeline designers can adopt directly. The benchmark organizes its evaluation along four independent axes. The first axis is an attack taxonomy covering 17 representative jailbreak techniques, giving you a standardized vocabulary for naming and tracking techniques across test runs and across teams. The second axis is a model matrix spanning 9 aligned LLMs, enabling apples-to-apples comparison of vulnerability across different systems. The third axis is a violation-category taxonomy built from 160 forbidden questions spanning 16 harm categories, including weapons, illegal activities, hate speech, and privacy violations, among others. The fourth axis is a defense evaluation matrix covering 8 defensive measures.

This four-axis design is deliberately modular. The violation-category taxonomy is especially important because it replaces ad hoc prompt selection with systematic coverage. Rather than choosing harmful prompts based on what testers happen to think of, you draw from defined categories to ensure no major harm type goes untested. The defense evaluation matrix transforms the core evaluation question from "does this attack work?" to "does this attack work against this defense, on this model, for this violation type?" — a substantially richer and more actionable question.

The practical takeaway from this architecture is: structure first. Before you run a single prompt, define your taxonomy of attacks, your taxonomy of harm categories, your target model set, and your candidate defenses. These four definitions form the scaffold from which the entire pipeline is built. Without this scaffold, results will be neither reproducible nor comparable to other teams' findings — and the interaction effects that matter most will remain invisible.

The RED-EVAL / RED-INSTRUCT Development Cycle

The RED-EVAL and RED-INSTRUCT work demonstrates something more ambitious than a benchmark: a complete, closed-loop adversarial development cycle. The pipeline moves through four distinct phases. First, attack generation: RED-EVAL employs the Chain of Utterances (CoU) technique, a multi-turn jailbreaking approach where each conversation turn nudges the model incrementally closer to a harmful response, rather than attempting a single large jailbreak in one shot. This technique achieved 65–73% manipulation success on GPT-4 and ChatGPT, and over 86% success on open-source models. Second, vulnerability benchmarking: the CoU attacks are systematically run against target models and all outcomes are recorded.

Third, training data collection: the harmful conversations produced during red-teaming are paired with human-written safe rewrites to create alignment training data. This step is the hinge of the entire cycle. Rather than discarding adversarial outputs as waste, the pipeline treats them as raw material. Fourth, fine-tuning for defense: training on this data produced the STARLING model, demonstrating that adversarial outputs can be converted directly into defensive training signal.

The organizational implication of this cycle is significant. Red-teaming and safety training are not separate activities belonging to different departments — they are two phases of a single feedback loop. The attacks you generate during red-teaming are valuable assets: curated, labeled, and ready to become training data for the next generation of safer models. Pipeline designers should build their data collection systems with this downstream reuse in mind from the very beginning, not as an afterthought once the red-team exercise is already complete.

Team Composition and Scope Definition

The Azure AI red-teaming guide addresses the human side of pipeline design. Effective red teams are deliberately heterogeneous. The guide recommends four distinct role types: domain experts, who understand what harmful outputs look like in the specific application context; security engineers, who bring knowledge of attack vectors from traditional software security; AI safety specialists, who understand model behavior patterns; and testers without domain expertise, who provide perspectives unconstrained by professional assumptions. The fourth role is counterintuitive but important: domain experts sometimes cannot see attack paths that are obvious to a naive user, precisely because their expertise filters out what appears to be nonsense at first glance.

Scope definition is equally critical and often conflated. The guide draws a clear distinction between two fundamentally different testing targets. Base model testing examines the model in isolation, before any system prompt or tool integration is applied. Application testing examines the model with its production system prompt, tool access, and retrieval systems in place. These two scopes answer different questions: base model testing reveals inherent model vulnerabilities independent of deployment context, while application testing reveals vulnerabilities that emerge specifically from the way the model has been deployed. Both are necessary; neither alone is sufficient. Conflating them — testing the application and drawing conclusions about the base model, or vice versa — is a common and consequential methodological error that leads teams to misattribute the source of a vulnerability.

Data Collection, Testing Modes, and Iteration

A red-teaming pipeline is only as good as its data collection discipline. The Azure AI guide recommends structured templates for recording each attack attempt: the attack input used, the model's response, the attack type, a severity rating, and a recommended mitigation. Severity ratings are not optional polish — without them it is impossible to prioritize which vulnerabilities to remediate first when engineering resources are constrained. Recommended mitigations ensure that each finding becomes a step toward a safer system rather than an isolated data point.

The guide also distinguishes two testing modes that serve different purposes in the pipeline. Open-ended exploration gives red-teamers the freedom to choose their own strategies and follow unexpected paths; this mode excels at discovery, surfacing attack types that structured testing would miss entirely because no one thought to define them in advance. Guided targeted testing assigns specific harm categories to specific testers, ensuring systematic coverage across the full violation-category taxonomy before sign-off. A mature pipeline uses both modes in sequence: open-ended exploration early in a test cycle to identify novel attack paths, followed by guided targeted testing to close coverage gaps.

Orchestrating the full pipeline means sequencing these phases deliberately: scope definition and team assembly first; then open-ended exploration; then guided targeted testing against the full violation-category taxonomy; then defense measurement against the candidate defensive measures; then synthesis and reporting. Each phase feeds the next. The attack outputs from red-teaming inform defense selection; defense measurement results feed back into the attack taxonomy and training data for the next cycle. The pipeline is not a straight line — it is a loop, and its value compounds with each iteration.

Key Insight: JailbreakRadar demonstrated that evaluating attacks and defenses in separate studies is a structural flaw, not merely a methodological preference. An attack achieving 90% success against an undefended model may drop to 10% against a simple defense — an interaction that siloed research is constitutionally unable to reveal. The most important thing a red-teaming pipeline must do is measure attacks and defenses together, not separately.
Common Misconception: Many practitioners treat base model testing and application testing as interchangeable or assume that a clean base model result implies a safe deployed application. They are distinct scopes that answer different questions. A base model that shows low vulnerability in isolation can become highly exploitable once a production system prompt, tool access, or retrieval system is layered on top of it — and vice versa. Always specify which scope you are testing before drawing any conclusions.
