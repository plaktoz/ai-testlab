Status Quo Bias

We prefer things to stay as they are

The Big Idea

Imagine your internet is slow, overpriced, and drops out every evening. You've complained about it for two years. But you still haven't switched providers. Why not? Switching feels like work. It feels risky. What if the new one is worse? Better the devil you know — so you do nothing.

Status Quo Bias = We treat the current situation as the safe default.
Any change from "the way things are" feels like a loss, even when staying put is clearly worse.

The status quo is whatever is already happening — your current job, your current habits, your current provider. Our brain codes staying the same as "neutral." But change always carries the mental price tag of potential loss. So we stay put far longer than makes sense.

The Trap: Staying Still When You Should Move

Status quo bias feels like caution. It disguises itself as wisdom. "If it ain't broke, don't fix it." But it works even when things ARE broke. The slow internet is broken. The outdated system is broken. The job that makes you miserable every day is broken. And still we stay.

The reason it feels so natural is that change requires mental effort — and our brains are lazy by design. Change also connects to loss aversion: any change feels more like a possible loss than a possible gain. The two biases team up to make staying put feel like the wise, safe choice when it isn't.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Organ Donation Opt-Out Countries
In countries where organ donation is opt-in (you must sign up), donation rates are low — around 10–20%. In countries where it is opt-out (you're a donor unless you say otherwise), rates jump above 90%. The people didn't change. Their values didn't change. Only the default did. Because of status quo bias, most people stick with whatever the default is — whether that default saves lives or not.

Example 2 — The Bad Internet Provider
You've been paying too much for slow internet for three years. You know a cheaper, faster provider exists. Your neighbour switched and loves it. But every time you think about switching, you imagine filling out forms, waiting for a technician, and possible downtime. The hassle feels bigger than the benefit — even though it's just a few hours of one-time effort for years of savings. Status quo bias exaggerates the cost of change and shrinks the benefit in your mind.
Example 3 — The Outdated System at Work
Many companies run on ancient software that was "state of the art" in 2005. Everyone knows a better system exists. Everyone knows the old one wastes hours each week. But upgrading means training, disruption, and the unfamiliar. "At least we know how this one works," people say — and the company stays stuck for another decade. Status quo bias keeps organisations using broken tools because familiar feels safe.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Notice the default. Ask yourself: am I doing this because it's genuinely the best option, or just because it's what's already happening? Name the status quo clearly.

Step 2 — Flip the question. Instead of "why should I change?", ask: "If I were starting fresh today, would I choose this?" If the answer is no, that gap is where status quo bias is trapping you.

Step 3 — Make change the default. Wherever possible, set up defaults in your favour. Automatic savings. Pre-scheduled reviews of contracts, subscriptions, and habits. Make the better choice the easy path.
