First Principles Thinking

Break things down to their true building blocks, then build back up from scratch.

The Big Idea

Imagine your friend says: "Bikes are expensive, so I can't get to school." A normal person says: "Yeah, bikes are expensive."

A first principles thinker asks: "Wait — what do I actually need? I need to move my body from home to school. Does it have to be a bike? Could I walk, skateboard, or ask someone to share a ride?"

First Principles Thinking = Stop copying other people's answers.
Break the problem all the way down to the basic true facts, then build your own answer from there.

The word "principle" just means a basic, true fact that can't be broken down further. Like: water is made of hydrogen and oxygen. That's a first principle of water. You can't break it down further.

The Opposite: Copycat Thinking

Most people think by analogy — they copy what others have done before. "Everyone uses a bike, so I need a bike." "Everyone goes to university, so I must too." "Rockets are built that way because that's how rockets are built."

Copycat thinking is fine for small, everyday things. But when you want to solve hard problems or create something new, you need first principles.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Elon Musk & SpaceX
In 2001, rockets cost $65 million each. Everyone said: "That's just what rockets cost." Musk asked: "What is a rocket actually made of? Metal, fuel, electronics." He calculated the raw materials cost only 2% of the price. So he built his own rockets and cut the cost by 10×. He didn't accept the price — he questioned it from the ground up.
Source: Farnam Street — First Principles
Example 2 — Making Lunch
You want a sandwich but you're out of bread. Analogy thinking: "No bread = no sandwich, I'm stuck." First principles thinking: "What is a sandwich? Something to hold filling that I can eat. Could I use lettuce leaves? A rice cake? Tortilla? Crackers?" You find a solution because you questioned the assumption that bread is required.
Example 3 — Saving Money
You think: "I can't save money — my rent and bills are too high." First principles asks: "What do I actually need each month to survive and be happy? Food, shelter, health, connection. Do all my current expenses serve those needs? Which ones are habits I copied from others?" You might find $300/month of spending that doesn't actually make you happier.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Name the assumption. What are you taking for granted? Write it down.

Step 2 — Ask "why is this true?" Keep asking "why?" until you hit a basic fact that can't be broken down further.

Step 3 — Rebuild. Starting only from those basic facts, what solution can you build?
