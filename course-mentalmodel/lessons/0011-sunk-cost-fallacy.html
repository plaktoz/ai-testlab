Sunk Cost Fallacy

Past investment shouldn't drive future decisions

The Big Idea

You're sitting in a movie theater. Thirty minutes in, the film is terrible — boring, confusing, and a waste of time. But you paid $15 for the ticket. So you stay. "I already paid," you think, "I can't just leave." You sit through two more miserable hours to avoid feeling like the $15 was wasted. But here's the thing: that $15 is gone no matter what you do. You can't get it back. The only question is: how do you want to spend the next two hours?

Sunk Cost Fallacy = Continuing to do something bad just because you've already spent time, money, or effort on it — even though that investment is gone forever.
A sunk cost is already paid. It should have zero influence on what you do next.

The smart move is to look only at the future: "From this moment forward, what gives me the most value?" The past investment is irrelevant. It's gone. Letting it control you is the fallacy.

The Trap: Throwing Good Money After Bad

The trap is painfully common. A company spends $50 million building a product nobody wants. Instead of stopping, they spend another $50 million because "we've come so far." A student spends four years studying a subject they hate and then spends the next forty years in a career they hate — because they "can't waste" the degree.

The feeling behind the fallacy is real and powerful: we hate feeling like something was wasted. Walking away feels like admitting defeat. But staying in a losing situation just to avoid that feeling is like drinking poison because you already bought it. The only rational question is: does continuing help me from here?

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Bad Movie You Sit Through
You pay for a movie ticket and the film is dreadful. You stay through the whole thing because you "paid for it." But the ticket cost is sunk — it's spent whether you leave at minute 30 or stay until the credits roll. Leaving frees you to do something you'd actually enjoy. Staying just multiplies the loss: now you've wasted both the money and the time. (Farnam Street)
Example 2 — The Wrong Career
Someone spends four years getting a law degree and realizes they despise legal work. They spend the next 20 years as an unhappy lawyer because "I can't throw away four years of law school." But those four years are already gone. Every year they stay is a new cost — a new choice being made — not a recovery of the old one. The question is only: what do I want my next year to look like?
Example 3 — The Failing Business Project
A startup has spent 18 months and $200,000 building an app that users keep abandoning. The team pushes on because of all the effort invested. In reality, the $200,000 is gone. Each new month costs more real money and real time. The right question isn't "did we already spend a lot?" — it's "does this product have a realistic path to success from today onward?"

How To Spot It (3 Signs)

Sign 1 — You say "I've already paid so much." When this phrase shows up in your reasoning, flag it immediately. Past payment is sunk. Remove it from the equation and re-evaluate the future on its own terms.

Sign 2 — You feel guilty even thinking about quitting. Guilt about past investment is a signal, not a decision rule. The question to ask is: "If I hadn't already invested anything, would I choose to start this now?" If no, that's your answer.

Sign 3 — You're staying to "not waste" rather than to gain. If your only reason for continuing is to justify the past, you're in the fallacy. Good reasons to continue are forward-looking: "this will still pay off," not "I've already put too much in to stop."
