Inertia

Objects in motion stay in motion — objects at rest stay at rest

The Big Idea

Imagine a big boulder sitting on flat ground. Getting it to move the first time is incredibly hard. You push and push. Then — slowly — it starts to roll. Now it takes much less effort to keep it going. In fact, if you stop pushing, it might just keep rolling on its own. That is inertia. Things keep doing what they're already doing.

Inertia = a thing in motion stays in motion; a thing at rest stays at rest.
Change requires a force — either to start something or to stop it.

Isaac Newton described this as his First Law of Motion. But it applies far beyond physics. Your habits have inertia. A business has inertia. Even a conversation has inertia. If you've been exercising every day for three months, stopping feels harder than continuing. If you haven't exercised in three months, starting feels enormous. The good news: you only have to push hard once. After that, momentum does the work.

The Trap: When Bad Things Keep Going Because Nobody Stops Them

Inertia isn't only positive. It also keeps bad things going. A company that built its fortune in one product keeps making that product long after the market moved on. Nobody wants to be the person who stops what the whole organisation has been doing for twenty years. The inertia of past success becomes the trap of future failure.

People stay in jobs they hate, relationships that don't work, and habits that hurt them — because changing direction requires energy, and doing nothing requires none. Recognising inertia is the first step to breaking it.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Daily Exercise Streak
A runner who hasn't missed a single morning run in ninety days finds it strange to skip. The habit has momentum. The alarm goes off, they lace up, they go — almost automatically. When they miss one day due to illness, the next day feels hard. Two days off and it's a real struggle. Three days and the habit feels broken. Inertia works both ways: motion builds motion, and rest builds rest. The key is to make the first push — and never let the streak die without restarting quickly.
Example 2 — The Failing Product Nobody Stops Making
A technology company built its business on CD-ROM software in the 1990s. By 2005, everyone was moving online. The company knew this. But they had factories, staff, supplier contracts, and an entire division dedicated to CDs. The inertia of the existing business kept them making CDs long after customers stopped buying them. Changing direction would have required firing people, closing factories, and admitting the old path was wrong. Inertia made staying still easier than changing — until it was too late.
Example 3 — Building Momentum in a New Language
Learning Spanish felt impossible for the first two weeks. Every session was a struggle. Every word felt like lifting a weight. But after six weeks of daily practice, something clicked. The learner started recognising words, forming simple sentences, understanding more each day. Continuing became easier than stopping. That is positive inertia: once you've built momentum in a direction, the system wants to keep moving. The hardest part of any learning journey is the very beginning — before inertia is on your side.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Identify your inertia. Look at your habits, routines, and projects. Which ones are moving in a good direction? Which are stuck? Inertia is keeping both groups exactly where they are.

Step 2 — Apply force at the start. For good habits, make the first step tiny and easy. Do two minutes of exercise today. Write one sentence. Make one phone call. Starting creates motion. Motion creates inertia.

Step 3 — Never fully stop a good streak. When life interrupts, restart immediately — even in a reduced form. A five-minute walk counts. One paragraph counts. Keep the motion alive so inertia stays on your side.
