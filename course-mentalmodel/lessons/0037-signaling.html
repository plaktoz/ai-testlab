Signaling

What you do signals who you are — even when words say otherwise

The Big Idea

Imagine a peacock. It has an enormous, heavy, impractical tail. That tail slows the peacock down. It makes the bird easier for predators to catch. Why on earth would evolution produce something so costly? Because the tail sends an unmistakable message to peahens: "I am so healthy, so strong, so genetically fit that I can survive even with this ridiculous handicap." Only a truly excellent bird could afford that tail. It is a signal — and the cost of the signal is what makes it believable.

Signaling = communicating something about yourself through costly actions, choices, or possessions.
The signal only works if it is hard to fake — easy signals are worthless because anyone can copy them.

Humans signal constantly. An expensive watch says "I have money." Volunteering says "I value this cause." A degree from a top university says "I worked hard enough and am smart enough to get in." The key insight is that the signal must carry a real cost — otherwise it is not a signal, it is just noise. Anyone can say "I'm trustworthy." Far fewer people will spend years building a reputation by keeping every promise.

The Opposite: Empty Talk

Words are cheap signals. "I'm committed to this project." "I really care about you." "This company puts customers first." These cost nothing to say. So they communicate almost nothing. Cheap talk is everywhere — in politics, advertising, and personal relationships. When you hear words without costly actions behind them, treat them as weak evidence. Watch what people do, not what they say. Actions are costly. Costs are credible.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The MBA Degree
An MBA from a top business school costs roughly $200,000 and two years of your life. Why would employers care? Partly for the knowledge — but mostly for the signal. Completing that degree proves you had the intelligence to be admitted, the work ethic to finish, and the financial resources or confidence to invest that much. The cost IS the signal. A free online course can teach the same material, but it doesn't signal the same thing, because anyone can take it with no sacrifice.
Example 2 — Volunteering Your Saturdays
When someone volunteers every Saturday at a food bank, they signal something real: they value community enough to give up free time consistently. That sacrifice is visible and verifiable. It costs them sleep, leisure, and weekends. So when they say "I care about helping others," you believe them — because the costly action already proved it. Compare this to someone who only says "I care about charity" but never acts. The words land with very different weight.
Example 3 — Luxury Brands and Status
A $15,000 watch tells time no better than a $30 watch. So why does anyone buy it? To signal wealth and status. The high price is the entire point — it proves to observers that the buyer can afford to spend that much on something purely decorative. Fashion brands understand this perfectly. They deliberately keep prices high to preserve the signal. If a luxury bag became cheap, it would lose its value as a signal almost immediately, because anyone could have one.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Ask what your actions signal. Think about the choices you make — where you spend time, money, and energy. Those choices are broadcasting a message about you right now. Do they signal what you actually want to signal? If not, change your actions before changing your words.

Step 2 — Ignore cheap signals in others. When evaluating people, ideas, or organizations, look past words and low-cost gestures. Focus on what they have actually done — what they have sacrificed, risked, or invested. Those costly actions are far more informative than any statement.

Step 3 — Invest in credible signals. If you want people to trust you, show up consistently over a long time. If you want to signal competence, build real skills and let the results speak. Cheap shortcuts — fake credentials, paid testimonials, empty promises — are fragile. Costly, genuine signals compound over time.
