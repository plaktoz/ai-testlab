Hyperbolic Discounting

We overvalue now and undervalue later

The Big Idea

Imagine someone offers you a choice: take $10 right now, or wait one week and get $12. Most people grab the $10. Even though $12 is clearly more money.

Why? Because the future feels blurry and far away. "Now" feels real and certain. Our brain puts a huge discount on anything that isn't happening immediately.

Hyperbolic Discounting = Our brain shrinks the value of future rewards.
The further away a reward is, the less exciting it feels right now — even if it's objectively better.

The word "hyperbolic" means the discount is very steep at first, then flattens out. You'd rather have $10 now than $12 in a week — but you'd happily wait for $12 in 53 weeks instead of $10 in 52 weeks. Same one-week gap, but suddenly you're patient. That's the weird shape of how our brains work.

The Trap: Living for Now

When "now" feels so much better than "later," we make choices that hurt our future selves. We eat the whole pizza tonight and plan to diet starting Monday. We scroll our phones instead of studying, because studying feels like future-you's problem. We skip saving money because retirement feels like it's a hundred years away.

The trap feels completely natural. The future you is basically a stranger. Why sacrifice comfort now for someone you've never met — even if that someone is you? Recognising this pull is the first step to escaping it.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Retirement Savings Gap
Most people know they should save for retirement. The math is simple — a little money set aside every month grows into a lot over decades. But most people don't start. Why? Because $200 saved today feels like a real, immediate sacrifice. The retirement nest egg 30 years away feels like a movie you'll watch someday — not something real. Hyperbolic discounting makes us feel the pain of saving now more sharply than the pleasure of security later.
Source: Farnam Street — Mental Models
Example 2 — The Procrastination Loop
You have a report due in two weeks. Every evening you think: "I'll start tomorrow." Tomorrow arrives. You think it again. The night before the deadline, panic kicks in. Suddenly the deadline is "now" — and now feels real. So now you finally act. The task didn't change. The deadline didn't move. What changed is that the consequence became immediate. Hyperbolic discounting kept you comfortable until "later" turned into "right now."
Example 3 — The Midnight Snack
You've been eating well all week. At 11 pm, the leftover cake in the fridge starts calling your name. You know eating it now will set back your health goals. But those goals feel abstract and far away. The cake is real. The cake is now. Your brain values the immediate pleasure of the cake far more than the distant pleasure of losing a kilo. That's hyperbolic discounting on a dessert-sized scale.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Name the future reward. Write down what you actually get if you wait — be specific. "I will have $3,000 saved by December" is more real to your brain than "save money."

Step 2 — Remove the immediate temptation. Don't fight willpower. Make the bad now-choice harder. Put your phone in another room. Delete the delivery app. Set up automatic savings so the money moves before you see it.

Step 3 — Make the future feel closer. Imagine yourself six months from now in vivid detail. Talk to your future self. This shrinks the psychological distance and makes future rewards feel more real and worth waiting for.
