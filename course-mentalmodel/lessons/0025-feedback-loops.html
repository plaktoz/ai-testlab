Feedback Loops

Outputs become new inputs — creating cycles that amplify or dampen themselves

The Big Idea

Hold a microphone too close to a speaker. The microphone picks up the speaker's sound, sends it to the amplifier, which makes it louder through the speaker, which the microphone picks up again — louder this time. Within a second you get a horrible screech. That's a feedback loop: the output circles back and becomes a new input, which creates a new output, and so on in a self-feeding cycle.

Feedback Loop = When the output of a system feeds back in as a new input, creating a cycle that either amplifies or dampens over time.
Positive loops snowball — they amplify. Negative loops stabilize — they dampen.

These two types work very differently. A positive feedback loop keeps adding to itself: more → more → more (until something stops it). A negative feedback loop self-corrects: too much → pulls back → returns to normal. Healthy systems use both. Your body temperature is regulated by a negative loop. A startup's viral growth is driven by a positive one.

The Opposite: One-Way Thinking

Most people think of cause and effect as a straight line: A causes B, full stop. They don't see that B might circle back and change A. This is called linear thinking — and it causes people to miss the snowball effects that shape real outcomes.

A small investment in daily exercise doesn't just improve your fitness slightly. It improves your energy, which improves your sleep, which reduces stress, which sharpens your focus, which makes you more productive, which gives you more time to exercise. Each benefit feeds the next. Ignoring the loop leads you to dramatically underestimate both the rewards of good habits and the damage of bad ones.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Social Media Likes
You post a photo and it gets 50 likes. The positive reaction encourages you to post more. You start tuning your content to what earns the most engagement. More engagement grows your following. More followers mean more likes on each post. More likes encourage more posting. The loop feeds itself. For creators, this can build a genuine audience. For everyone else, it can pull toward addictive patterns — the loop doesn't care which direction it's running you.
Example 2 — Stock Market Panic
Prices drop slightly. Nervous investors sell to cut their losses. More selling drives prices down further. Falling prices scare more investors, who sell even more. This is a positive feedback loop — not "positive" as in good, but as in self-amplifying. The cycle keeps running until something breaks it: a trading halt, a coordinated government intervention, or investors simply running out of shares to sell. Panics feed on themselves.
Example 3 — Learning to Read
A child learns a handful of words. Those words let her read simple books. Simple books teach her new words. New words let her tackle harder books. Harder books introduce even more vocabulary. The better she gets, the faster she improves — because each gain makes the next gain easier. This is a powerful positive feedback loop. It explains why early investment in reading pays dividends for life, and why kids who fall behind early often keep falling further behind.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Identify the loop. In any system you're examining, ask: does this output circle back to become a new input? Trace the chain.

Step 2 — Label it. Is this loop amplifying (positive) or stabilizing (negative)? Understanding the type tells you whether it will snowball or self-correct.

Step 3 — Act on it deliberately. Find and feed the good positive loops in your life. Spot the bad ones and break them early — they're much easier to stop when small than when they've already snowballed.
