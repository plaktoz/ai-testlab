Regression to the Mean

Extreme results tend to drift back toward average over time

The Big Idea

A basketball player has the game of his life — 42 points, every shot dropping, crowd going wild. The next night he scores 17 points and plays like his usual self. Did he "slump"? Not really. He returned to his normal level. The 42-point game was a statistical outlier — an unusually good result given his real skill. The next game, the randomness that gave him that great night wasn't there, so he drifted back toward his true average. That drift is called regression to the mean.

Regression to the Mean = extreme results — very good or very bad — tend to be followed by results closer to average.
This happens naturally, through statistics, not because of any intervention or change.

Sir Francis Galton noticed this in the 1880s when studying height. Very tall fathers had tall sons — but sons who were slightly shorter than their fathers. Very short fathers had short sons — but slightly taller than their fathers. The extreme result of one generation drifted back toward the average of the population. The same pattern appears in test scores, business performance, sports, health, and almost any measured outcome that has any randomness in it.

The Opposite: The Intervention Illusion

The dangerous flip side is when we make a change after an extreme result, the result naturally improves, and we credit the change. A coach shouts at a player who had a terrible game. Next game, the player plays better. The coach concludes: "My harsh talk worked!" But the improvement might have happened anyway — the terrible game was an outlier, and regression would have pulled the player back toward normal regardless. Conversely, when we praise someone for an exceptional performance and they perform more averagely next, we wrongly conclude: "Praise must make people worse." This is one of the most common sources of false lessons in management, medicine, and everyday life.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Rookie of the Year Who Disappoints
A baseball player has a stunning debut season — hitting above his natural talent level partly due to good luck on close plays and favorable randomness. He wins Rookie of the Year. The following season he performs more like a solid-but-not-spectacular player. Sports journalists call it a "sophomore slump." But there is no slump — just a return to his true average after an unusually lucky year. Regression to the mean is often misread as a decline.
Example 2 — Scolding vs. Praising Employees
A manager notices that employees who had a terrible quarter tended to perform better the next quarter — regardless of whether they were scolded or supported. Meanwhile, employees who had an exceptional quarter tended to perform more averagely the next quarter — regardless of praise or reward. The manager incorrectly concludes: "Scolding works; praising backfires." In reality, both groups just regressed toward their individual means. The interventions had little to do with it.
Example 3 — "Miracle" Health Treatments
People seek out new treatments — diets, pills, alternative therapies — when their symptoms are at their worst. Then their symptoms improve. They attribute the improvement to the treatment. But people naturally seek help at the peak of their illness, which is an extreme result. Bodies often recover on their own. The improvement they experienced would have happened with or without the treatment — it was regression to the mean. This is why proper clinical trials with control groups are essential: to separate real effects from natural regression.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Ask whether an extreme result has randomness in it. When you see a very high or very low result, ask: "Is there any chance element here, or is this purely skill?" The more randomness is involved, the more strongly the next result will regress toward average. Pure skill — like solving a math problem — doesn't regress. Performance with luck in it — like a sports score — almost always does.

Step 2 — Don't confuse natural regression with the effect of your actions. When you intervene after a very bad result and things improve, be honest: would they have improved anyway? Use comparison groups, track baselines, and look for patterns across many cases — not just one memorable before-and-after story.

Step 3 — Set expectations based on averages, not peaks. When hiring, investing, or evaluating people, look at their average performance over time — not their best moment. A single extraordinary result is the least informative data point you can use. A consistent record of solid average performance is far more predictive of future results.
