Hanlon's Razor

Don't assume malice when a simple mistake explains it.

The Big Idea

Someone cuts you off in traffic. Your first instinct might be: "What a horrible person — they did that on purpose!" But did they? Maybe they didn't see you. Maybe they're rushing to a hospital. Maybe they just weren't paying attention. Hanlon's Razor says: before you decide someone is evil, check if they're just being human.

Hanlon's Razor = "Never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity."
In plain English: most of the time when someone hurts you, they didn't mean to — they just made a mistake.

Named after Robert J. Hanlon, who popularized the phrase in the 1980s. This razor is a filter: before you get angry at someone for being evil, ask if carelessness or ignorance explains the situation just as well. It usually does.

The Opposite: Assuming Malice

Assuming malice is when you decide someone acted against you on purpose without enough evidence. Your boss gave you critical feedback — they must be trying to push you out. Your friend arrived late — they clearly don't respect you.

Assuming malice is emotionally exhausting and usually wrong. It turns you into someone who sees enemies everywhere. It destroys trust, ruins relationships, and wastes energy that could go toward actually solving problems.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Unreplied Email
You send your colleague an important email and they don't reply for three days. Your mind races: "They're ignoring me. They're plotting against me. They want me to fail." But when you finally ask, the truth is simpler: their inbox was flooded, they saw your email and mentally noted it, then forgot to respond. No malice. Just a very common human mistake. Hanlon's Razor would have saved you three days of unnecessary stress.

Example 2 — The Friend Who Forgot
You told your best friend about an important event two weeks ago. On the day, they don't show up or even send a message. You feel betrayed. But when you call, you find out they simply forgot — life got busy and it slipped their mind. They feel terrible about it. Assuming malice would have ended a good friendship over an accident.
Example 3 — The Critical Boss
Your manager gives you blunt feedback on a project, pointing out several mistakes. It stings. You start thinking: "They're targeting me. They want me gone." But step back — is it more likely that your manager is running a personal campaign against you, or that they're just doing their job and weren't very tactful about it? Hanlon's Razor points to the second explanation almost every time.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Notice the hot feeling. When someone does something that upsets you, catch yourself before you react. That anger is a signal to pause and think.

Step 2 — Ask the Hanlon question. "Could this have happened through carelessness, forgetfulness, or a mistake — rather than deliberate intent?" If yes, start there.

Step 3 — Respond to the most likely explanation. If it was probably a mistake, respond calmly and address the situation directly. Save accusations of bad intent for cases where you have clear evidence.
