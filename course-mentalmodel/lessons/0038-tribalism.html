Tribalism

Loyalty to our group warps how we see everything

The Big Idea

For almost all of human history, we lived in small groups of 50 to 150 people. Your tribe fed you, protected you, and gave your life meaning. Helping your tribe and distrusting strangers was smart. It kept you alive. That ancient wiring is still running inside every human brain today — even though we now live in cities of millions and interact with people from everywhere on earth.

Tribalism = the automatic tendency to favor your own group and be suspicious of outsiders.
It distorts judgment: your group's actions feel justified; the same actions by another group feel wrong.

Tribes today wear many costumes: sports teams, political parties, companies, nationalities, religions, neighborhoods. The clothing changes but the brain mechanism is the same. The danger is not that you belong to groups — that is natural and good. The danger is that tribal loyalty can replace clear thinking. When you stop asking "what is actually true here?" and start asking "what does my group believe?" — your reasoning has been hijacked.

The Opposite: Clear Thinking

Clear thinkers deliberately ask: "Would I judge this action the same way if the other team did it?" They seek out information that challenges their tribe's view. They maintain friendships across group lines. They reward honest critics within their group rather than silencing them. This is hard — the brain fights back with discomfort, guilt, and social pressure. But it is the only way to see the world closer to how it actually is.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Sports Fans and Fouls
In a football match, your team's striker elbows an opponent. "He barely touched him," you think — "the other guy is being dramatic." Five minutes later, the opposition striker does the exact same thing to your player. "That was a disgraceful foul! The referee must be blind!" The action was identical. Your judgment was completely different. This is tribalism operating in real time — your loyalty to your team is filtering the raw facts through a lens of "us vs. them."
Example 2 — Corporate Loyalty
An employee at a big company sees a major strategic mistake being made by senior leadership. But instead of speaking up, they defend the decision in meetings — because criticizing the company feels like betraying the tribe. Meanwhile, employees at a competing company would see the mistake immediately and clearly. Tribal loyalty inside organizations silences honest feedback, protects bad decisions, and causes companies to fail at problems they could have solved if people had been willing to think freely.
Example 3 — Political Partisans
Studies consistently show that people evaluate the same policy very differently depending on which political party proposed it. The same healthcare plan is "sensible reform" when your party suggests it and "dangerous overreach" when the opposition does. The policy didn't change. Only the label changed. This is not stupidity — it is tribalism doing exactly what it evolved to do: protect the group. But in modern democracy, it prevents people from finding the best solutions to real problems.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Name your tribes. Make a list of the groups you belong to that trigger strong loyalty: team, party, company, nationality, religion. Awareness is the first defense. You cannot fight a bias you have not named.

Step 2 — Apply the flip test. When you are judging an action, ask: "If the other group did this exact same thing, would I judge it the same way?" If the answer is no, tribalism is distorting your thinking. Try to find the most honest answer regardless of who did it.

Step 3 — Seek critics inside your tribe. Every group has members who question and push back. Seek them out. They are usually the most valuable thinkers in the group. A tribe that tolerates honest internal criticism is far more likely to make good decisions than one that demands loyalty above truth.
