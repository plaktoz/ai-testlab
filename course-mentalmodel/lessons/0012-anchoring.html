Anchoring

The first number you hear shapes all your later judgments

The Big Idea

You walk into a clothing store. The first jacket you see is priced at $500. You frown — that's expensive. Then you spot another jacket for $200. Suddenly $200 feels like a bargain! But is $200 actually a good deal for a jacket? Maybe not. You were just tricked by the first number — the anchor. It pulled your sense of "normal" toward $500, making $200 look cheap by comparison.

Anchoring = The first number (or piece of information) you encounter becomes a mental "anchor" that pulls all your later judgments toward it.
Your brain is trying to save time by using the first data point as a reference — even when that reference is random or manipulated.

The sneaky part is that anchors work even when we know they're irrelevant. Once a number is in your head, it's hard to shake. Salespeople, negotiators, and marketers know this and use it deliberately.

The Trap: Stuck to the First Number

The trap springs in every negotiation, every purchase, and many everyday judgments. A car dealer shows you the sticker price first — say, $40,000 — then offers it to you for $35,000. You feel like you're getting $5,000 off. But the sticker price was set specifically to make $35,000 feel like a deal. Your entire frame of reference has been captured by that first number.

The danger isn't just in shopping. When a manager gives a performance estimate of 30% growth before a meeting, every number the team proposes will cluster around 30%. When a doctor mentions a 10% survival rate before describing treatment options, patients make different choices than if they'd heard a 90% survival rate first — even though both facts can be true at once. The anchor sets the stage, and we can't easily step off it.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Kahneman's Spinning Wheel
Psychologist Daniel Kahneman ran an experiment: he had people spin a rigged wheel that stopped at either 10 or 65. Then he asked them unrelated questions — like "What percentage of African countries are in the United Nations?" People who spun 65 gave much higher estimates than people who spun 10. The wheel number was completely irrelevant, but it anchored their thinking anyway. This finding became one of the most replicated results in behavioral economics. (Farnam Street)
Example 2 — Salary Negotiation
When negotiating a salary, whoever names a number first sets the anchor. If a candidate says "$95,000" first, the whole conversation happens around that number. If the company says "$60,000" first, the candidate is now fighting upward from a low anchor. Research consistently shows that the first number in a negotiation has an outsized influence on the final outcome, even among experienced negotiators.
Example 3 — Restaurant Menus
High-end restaurants often place one extremely expensive item at the top of the menu — a $120 steak. Most people won't order it, but that's not the point. It makes every other item on the menu feel reasonably priced by comparison. The $45 pasta suddenly seems sensible next to the $120 steak. Remove the anchor dish, and suddenly $45 feels expensive again.

How To Spot It (3 Signs)

Sign 1 — You're judging something by comparing it to the first number you heard. When you find yourself thinking "well, compared to X, this is reasonable," ask: was X a fair reference point, or was it placed there to influence you?

Sign 2 — You're negotiating and the other side spoke first. Whoever sets the first number in a negotiation has anchored the conversation. Before accepting their frame, pause and ask: what number would I propose if I hadn't heard theirs?

Sign 3 — Something feels like a "deal" only in context. $200 is not inherently cheap. It is only cheap next to $500. Remove the anchor and ask: is this actually a good price on its own merits?
