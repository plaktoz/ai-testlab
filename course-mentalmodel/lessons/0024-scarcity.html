Scarcity

Limited resources create value, competition, and trade-offs

The Big Idea

Imagine a concert where 1,000 people want tickets but there are only 100 seats. What happens? People line up overnight. Prices shoot up. Resellers appear and charge triple. Some fans miss out entirely. All of that drama comes from one simple fact: there is less of something than people want. That gap between supply and desire is called scarcity — and it changes everything.

Scarcity = There will never be enough of everything for everyone. Because resources are limited, every choice has a cost.
When something is scarce, people compete for it, its value rises, and decisions become harder.

Scarcity is the central problem that economics exists to solve. It's not just about money or food. Time is scarce — you have 24 hours and that's it. Attention is scarce — you can only focus on one thing at a time. Even trust and goodwill are scarce. Understanding scarcity helps you see why people compete, why prices rise, and why every "yes" is also a "no" to something else.

The Opposite: Assuming Abundance

The opposite mistake is treating things as unlimited when they aren't. "I'll do it later." "There's always more budget." "I can always catch up." These phrases assume the resource is abundant — that it will always be there when you need it.

Treating scarce things as abundant leads to waste and regret. Time is the most irreplaceable resource — it cannot be saved, borrowed, or replenished. Businesses that don't track scarce resources like cash flow, skilled workers, or customer attention often run out before they realize the problem.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Your Attention
Every app on your phone is fighting for a piece of your day. Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, email, and text messages all compete for the same scarce resource: your attention. You only have about 16 waking hours. That scarcity is precisely why app designers spend billions of dollars engineering features that capture those minutes. Your attention is so scarce and so valuable that entire industries exist to monetize it.
Example 2 — Water Rights in California
In parts of California, farmers still argue over water rights written into contracts 100 years ago. In some countries, women and children walk hours each day just to collect water for their families. The same molecule that gets "wasted" washing cars in one place is life-or-death somewhere else. The difference is scarcity: where water is abundant, it has low value; where it's scarce, people fight, trade, and travel for it.
Example 3 — The Skilled-Worker Shortage
In recent years, millions of job openings sat unfilled even as companies raised wages dramatically. Nurses, electricians, software engineers, and airline pilots were all scarce. Companies competed with signing bonuses, free training, and remote-work flexibility. When skilled workers become scarce, the balance of power shifts — workers gain leverage and can demand more. Scarcity doesn't just affect goods; it governs the job market too.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Name the scarce thing. In any situation, ask: what resource is actually limited here? Time? Money? People? Attention? Be specific.

Step 2 — See the trade-off. If you spend that resource here, what do you give up? Every decision to use a scarce resource is also a decision to not use it for something else.

Step 3 — Allocate deliberately. Put your scarce resource where it creates the most value. Don't let it leak away through habit, distraction, or low-priority tasks.
