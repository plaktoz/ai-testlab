Availability Heuristic

What comes to mind easily feels more common than it is

The Big Idea

After watching a documentary about shark attacks, people suddenly feel afraid to swim in the ocean. But sharks kill fewer than ten people worldwide per year. Cars kill over a million. The fear isn't based on the real numbers. It's based on how vividly the shark attack played in their mind.

Availability Heuristic = We judge how likely something is by how easily we can think of an example.
If examples come to mind quickly and vividly, our brain says "that must happen a lot."

The psychologist Daniel Kahneman named and described this mental shortcut. It made sense when we lived in small tribes — if you saw a lion yesterday, lions were genuinely common nearby. But today, the news and social media flood us with vivid, dramatic, rare events — and our ancient brain still treats them as everyday facts.

The Trap: Confusing Memorable With Likely

Dramatic events — fires, plane crashes, shark bites, shark attacks, violent crimes — get covered in the news precisely because they are rare and shocking. The very fact that something makes the front page is evidence that it doesn't happen often. But our brain uses the ease of recall as a signal for frequency. The more you've heard about it, the more common it feels — whether it is common or not.

The mundane killers — heart disease, car accidents, slipping in the bathroom — barely register. They're too common and too boring to make headlines. So we underestimate the risks we face every day, and overestimate the risks we see on screens.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Plane Crashes vs. Car Crashes
Flying is one of the safest ways to travel. You are roughly 95 times more likely to die in a car crash than on a plane. But a single plane crash makes global news for days. It has names, faces, wreckage footage. Car deaths happen every minute somewhere in the world — and they barely make local news. Because plane crashes are so available in memory (vivid, recent, emotionally charged), people overestimate their danger and underestimate the road.

Example 2 — Crime Rates After a Robbery
Your friend's house gets broken into. Suddenly the neighbourhood feels dangerous. You find yourself double-checking locks, avoiding certain streets, telling others to be careful. But one robbery doesn't mean crime rates actually went up. Your brain updated its estimate of local crime based on one vivid, personal story — not on statistics. A robbery you heard about directly is highly available in memory, so it tricks your brain into thinking it's common.
Example 3 — Sharks vs. Heart Disease
Ask someone to name things that can kill you. Most people say: sharks, snakes, plane crashes, fires, murders. Almost nobody says: "sitting too much," "poor diet," or "chronic stress." But heart disease kills about 18 million people every year. Sharks kill fewer than ten. The dramatic killers are memorable. The quiet killers are invisible. Availability heuristic keeps our attention on rare dangers while the real ones sneak past.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Notice the vivid story. Whenever a dramatic event shapes your view of how common something is, pause. Ask: did I just see this in the news, or do I actually know the real numbers?

Step 2 — Look up the base rate. Base rates are the real frequency of something over a large population. Search for the actual statistics. Numbers override anecdotes. Reality is usually less dramatic than your news feed.

Step 3 — Weigh the boring risks too. Ask: "What dangers are so common they're invisible to me?" Everyday risks — diet, sleep, driving — deserve more attention than we usually give them.
