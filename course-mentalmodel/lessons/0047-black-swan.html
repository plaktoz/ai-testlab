Black Swan

Rare, extreme events that nobody predicted — but that change everything

The Big Idea

For thousands of years, everyone in Europe believed all swans were white. It was so obvious it wasn't even a theory — it was just a fact. Then, in 1697, explorers reached Australia and found black swans. Everything they "knew" with certainty was wrong. One sighting destroyed a belief held for centuries.

Black Swan = an event that is unpredictable, has massive impact, and — after the fact — seems like it should have been obvious.
You can't predict them. You can only build resilience to survive them.

Philosopher and risk analyst Nassim Taleb popularised this term in his 2007 book The Black Swan. His insight: the most important events in history are the ones nobody saw coming. The rise of the internet. The fall of the Berlin Wall. The 2008 financial crisis. COVID-19. After each of these, experts lined up to explain exactly why it was inevitable. But before? Nobody had it in their models. The lesson is not to predict Black Swans — it's to stop pretending you can, and instead build systems that survive them.

The Trap: Thinking You Can Predict the Unpredictable

The human brain is built to find patterns. We are deeply uncomfortable with "I don't know." So we build forecasts, run models, and produce confident predictions — even for things that are genuinely unknowable. When a Black Swan hits, we immediately explain why we should have seen it coming. This is called hindsight bias, and it makes us think we're better predictors than we are.

The danger is overconfidence. If you put all your money in one place, one career, one relationship, one plan — a single Black Swan can wipe you out. Diversification and resilience are not boring caution. They are the rational response to living in a world where rare, massive events are a certainty — even if you can't know when or what they will be.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Restaurant That Bet on Dine-In Only
A successful restaurant chain spent 2019 expanding aggressively. They opened twelve new locations, signed long leases, hired hundreds of staff, and built every location around the dine-in experience. They had no delivery capability, no takeaway infrastructure, and no online ordering. In early 2020, COVID-19 forced restaurants to close their dining rooms. The chain that had bet everything on dine-in had no backup. They went bankrupt within months. The pandemic was a Black Swan — unpredictable, massive, and devastating to those with no resilience.
Example 2 — The 2008 Financial Crisis
Major banks and financial institutions had complex models predicting the likelihood of mortgage defaults. Those models assumed markets followed a normal distribution — that extreme events were astronomically unlikely. They were spectacularly wrong. When the US housing market collapsed in 2007–2008, the ripple effects wiped out trillions of dollars and triggered a global recession. The models couldn't predict it because they were built on the assumption that the past predicts the future. Black Swans break that assumption. The models said it was a one-in-a-million-year event. It happened in our lifetime.
Example 3 — The Rise of the Internet
In the early 1990s, almost nobody predicted that the internet would reshape global commerce, communication, media, and politics within twenty years. The companies that adapted early — Amazon, Google — became the most valuable on Earth. The companies that didn't — bookshops, travel agents, video rental chains, newspapers — were destroyed or severely diminished. Looking back, every business writer explains why the internet's dominance was "obvious." At the time, almost none of them saw it. Black Swans are only obvious in hindsight.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Stop trying to predict. You cannot forecast Black Swans. Stop spending energy building overly precise predictions for an unpredictable future. Instead, acknowledge that big surprises will happen — you just don't know what or when.

Step 2 — Build resilience. Keep a financial buffer. Don't bet everything on one outcome. Diversify your income, your relationships, your skills. A system that survives shocks is worth more than one that's optimised for a future that may never arrive.

Step 3 — Be suspicious of confident forecasts. When someone tells you something big is definitely going to happen — or definitely isn't — remember the black swans. Treat extreme confidence in complex predictions as a warning sign, not a reason to relax.
