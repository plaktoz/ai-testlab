Power Laws

A tiny few are vastly more impactful than everything else combined

The Big Idea

You've heard of the 80/20 rule: 80% of results come from 20% of causes. But power laws are often far more extreme than that. In the music industry, the top 1% of artists earn more than the other 99% combined. The top 10 websites get more traffic than the next million combined. The richest 1% of people hold more wealth than the bottom 50% of the entire world population. This is not 80/20 — this is a power law: a tiny few are 10×, 100×, or even 1,000× larger than the rest.

Power Law = a distribution where a small number of items are vastly larger than all others.
The top are not a little better — they are orders of magnitude bigger. Average is nearly irrelevant.

In a normal distribution — a bell curve — most things cluster near the average. A few are a bit above average, a few are a bit below. Average is a useful concept. But power law distributions don't look like that. They look like a hockey stick: a long flat tail of countless small items, and a sharp spike at the very top. The "average" item in a power law distribution tells you almost nothing useful.

The Pareto Principle (also called the 80/20 rule) is one expression of this — named after Vilfredo Pareto, who observed that 80% of Italy's land was owned by 20% of its people. But real power laws go further. Venture capital returns follow a power law: one investment in a hundred can return more than the other ninety-nine combined. Understanding this changes how you think about searching for, evaluating, and choosing options.

The Opposite: The Bell Curve

A bell curve (normal distribution) is what we expect by default. Human heights, test scores, daily temperatures — these follow bell curves. Most values are near the middle. Extreme outliers are rare. In a bell curve world, average is a useful and meaningful number. A teacher who knows the class average knows something real about the class.

The mistake is to apply bell-curve thinking to power law domains. If you evaluate job candidates and assume the difference between good and great is modest and linear, you will radically underpay for the best hire. If you assume your next business investment will return "average," you will miss the one investment that could have paid for everything else. In power law domains, the difference between top and average is not incremental — it is categorical.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Venture Capital
A venture capital firm invests in 100 startups. Seventy fail completely. Twenty return modest amounts. Nine return 2–5× the investment. One — maybe Uber, Airbnb, or Google — returns 1,000×. That single investment pays back the entire fund many times over. This is why venture capitalists don't try to avoid failure — they expect it. Their strategy only works because of power laws: one winner can outweigh a hundred losers. Optimizing for the average return is exactly the wrong strategy.
Example 2 — Music Streaming
Over 100 million songs are available on Spotify. The top 1% of artists — roughly one million songs — capture over 90% of all streams. The vast majority of songs on the platform are listened to fewer than 100 times total. Some are listened to zero times. Yet the top 100 songs in any given week will have billions of streams each. This isn't a quirk of streaming — it reflects a fundamental pattern in human attention: we focus it on a tiny number of things, creating enormous inequality in outcomes.
Example 3 — Company Value Creation
The top 5 companies by market capitalization — typically Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Amazon, Alphabet — are worth more than the bottom 2,000 companies in the S&P 500 combined. One company, at its peak, can be worth more than the entire GDP of most countries. This is not because the top 5 work harder or are cleverer than everyone else. It is because technology, brand, and network effects create compounding advantages that produce power law outcomes in business value.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Identify Power Law Domains. Ask yourself: "Is this a domain where the best option is 100× better than the average — or just 10% better?" Software, investing, talent, attention, and media tend to follow power laws. Physical goods, hourly labor, and local services tend to follow bell curves. Different domains demand different strategies.

Step 2 — Search Harder for the Best. In a bell curve world, a good-enough option is fine. In a power law world, average options are nearly worthless by comparison to the best. Spend disproportionate effort finding the top option. The best employee, the best investment, the best partner, the best tool. The search cost is worth paying because the gap between best and average is enormous.

Step 3 — Accept Failure as the Cost of Finding Winners. In power law domains, you will have many more failures than successes. That's the math. A venture fund that never fails is probably avoiding the high-risk, high-reward bets where all the value lives. A person who only makes safe bets misses the life-changing wins. Calibrate your tolerance for failure to match the power law nature of the domain you're in.
