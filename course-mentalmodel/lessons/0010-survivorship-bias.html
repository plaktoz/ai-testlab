Survivorship Bias

We only see the winners — the losers are invisible

The Big Idea

Imagine you walk into a used bookstore and see a shelf of old books from the 1950s. Every single book is beautifully written and fascinating. You think: "Wow, people in the 1950s were amazing writers." But wait — there were thousands of terrible books written then too. They just got thrown away. You're only seeing the survivors.

Survivorship Bias = We draw conclusions from the winners we can see, while forgetting all the losers we can't see.
The failures didn't disappear — they just stopped being visible, so our brain forgets to include them.

This tricks us into thinking success is easier or more likely than it really is. We copy strategies from winners without knowing those same strategies led thousands of others straight to failure.

The Trap: Learning From Survivors Only

The trap is everywhere. We read books by successful CEOs and copy their morning routines — but we don't read books by the hundreds of CEOs who had the same routines and still failed. We hear about the startup that raised millions — but not the 90% that ran out of money quietly and closed.

The failures are silent. Nobody writes a bestseller called "How I Failed and Lost Everything." So all the lessons flowing into your brain come from the tiny fraction of winners — and you build a wildly distorted picture of how hard success actually is.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Abraham Wald and the WWII Planes
During World War II, the military studied bullet holes on planes returning from battle. They planned to add armor where the holes were. Mathematician Abraham Wald pointed out the error: those holes showed where a plane could be hit and still fly home. The planes hit in other spots — engines, cockpits — never made it back. The military was only seeing survivors. They added armor to the places with no holes. (Farnam Street)
Example 2 — The Startup Dream
You read about a college dropout who started a company in a garage and became a billionaire. Inspiring! But for every one of those stories, there are thousands of college dropouts who started garage companies and spent five years broke before giving up. Those people don't get magazine covers. Their silence makes the path look far clearer than it is.
Example 3 — Old Buildings and "Quality"
People often say old buildings are built better than new ones. But that's survivorship bias — the poorly built old buildings crumbled decades ago. What's left standing today is only the best construction from the past. We compare the survivors of the past to the average of the present, which isn't a fair comparison at all.

How To Spot It (3 Signs)

Sign 1 — You're only reading success stories. Whenever you're about to copy a strategy, ask: "How many people tried this and failed?" If you can't find the failures, you may only be seeing survivors.

Sign 2 — Something feels easier than it should be. If a path to success seems straightforward — just do what the winners did — survivorship bias is probably hiding the full picture. Real success usually involves a lot of invisible failure.

Sign 3 — The data is missing something. Think about who is not in the room, not in the study, not in the story. The absence of failure data is itself a clue that you're only looking at survivors.
