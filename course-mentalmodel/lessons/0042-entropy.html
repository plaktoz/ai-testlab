Entropy

Things naturally fall into disorder — order requires constant effort

The Big Idea

You spend Saturday morning cleaning your room until it is perfect — clothes folded, desk clear, floor spotless. Then you live in it for two weeks. Without any special effort, without any disasters, the socks end up on the floor, the desk fills with paper, and the clear floor disappears under a pile of bags. Nobody sabotaged you. You didn't decide to make a mess. The room just drifted back into disorder on its own. That drift is entropy. It is not bad luck — it is physics.

Entropy = the natural tendency of any system to move from order toward disorder over time.
Creating or maintaining order always costs energy. Left alone, everything falls apart.

The Second Law of Thermodynamics states that in any closed system, entropy — disorder — always increases. This is one of the most universal laws in all of science. It applies to physical systems (heat spreads from hot to cold, never the other way), but the principle captures something true about nearly every system humans care about: relationships, businesses, health, cities, codebases, gardens. Holding things together takes constant energy. Stop applying that energy, and disorder wins by default.

The Opposite: The Myth of Maintenance-Free Systems

The dangerous fantasy is that once you build something good, it will stay good. You hired a great team — so the culture will maintain itself. You got fit last year — so your fitness will hold. You launched a great product — so customers will keep coming. None of these are true. Cultures drift without active reinforcement. Fitness fades without regular exercise. Customers churn without ongoing engagement. The universe does not offer maintenance-free systems. Everything decays unless fed with energy.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Your Phone's Storage
You buy a new phone with plenty of storage. You don't plan to fill it up. But screenshots accumulate, apps multiply, cached data builds up, old videos never get deleted. Two years later, the phone is almost full and runs slowly. You did nothing wrong — entropy did it. Information systems drift toward clutter and redundancy without active maintenance. Cleaning out a digital system is exactly as necessary as cleaning a physical one, and it needs to happen on a regular schedule for the same reason.
Example 2 — A Garden Without Weeding
A gardener plants neat rows of vegetables in spring. By midsummer, without regular weeding, fast-growing weeds fill every gap, compete for nutrients, and begin choking the crops. The garden tends toward a wild field, not toward order. The gardener cannot plant once and walk away — maintaining the garden against the pull of entropy is the actual ongoing job. This is true of every system you want to keep ordered: it requires recurring, active effort, not a one-time creation event.
Example 3 — A Friendship That Fades
You and a close friend move to different cities. At first you call weekly. Then monthly. Then you go months without speaking, meaning to reach out but not quite doing it. One year later, the friendship feels distant and awkward, even though nothing bad happened. Relationships are not static structures — they are ongoing processes. Without energy input (calls, visits, shared experiences), they naturally drift toward distance and then silence. Entropy affects human connections just as it affects rooms and gardens.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Accept that decay is the default. Stop being surprised when things fall apart without your attention. The real question is not "why did this decline?" but "what energy am I putting in to prevent the natural decline?" If the answer is "none," decline is the expected result, not a mystery.

Step 2 — Build maintenance into your plans. When you start any project — a relationship, a habit, a business, a codebase — plan the ongoing maintenance from day one. Budget time and energy for it. Systems that don't have a maintenance plan degrade on a schedule. Systems with a maintenance plan can stay ordered indefinitely.

Step 3 — Do small maintenance often rather than large repairs rarely. A little weeding every week is far less work than clearing a completely overgrown garden once a year. A short check-in call every month prevents the awkward silence that requires a full relationship repair six months later. Regular small inputs of energy are far more efficient than large emergency restoration efforts.
