Mere Exposure Effect

Familiarity breeds liking — just seeing something more makes you like it more

The Big Idea

Think about a song you heard for the first time and thought was just OK. Then it played again on the radio. And again. And again. After the fifth time, you realized you were humming it. By the tenth time, it was one of your favorites. The music didn't change. The quality didn't improve. The only thing that changed was how many times you heard it. That is the Mere Exposure Effect at work.

Mere Exposure Effect = simply seeing or hearing something more times makes you like it more — with no other interaction required.
Familiarity creates positive feelings, even below the level of conscious awareness.

Psychologist Robert Zajonc discovered this in the 1960s. He showed people images — faces, shapes, symbols — some many times and some only once. Then he asked how much they liked each one. People consistently preferred the images they had seen more often, even when they had no memory of seeing them before. The brain treats familiarity as a signal of safety. "I've seen this before and nothing bad happened" — so it feels good. Advertisers have understood this for decades and run the same ads again and again on purpose.

The Opposite: The Strange and Unfamiliar

Our first reaction to something new and unfamiliar is often mild discomfort or suspicion. That is also hardwired — novelty can mean danger. But this means our first impressions of people, ideas, foods, and places are systematically biased against them just for being new. The fix is not to ignore first impressions, but to know that they are being dragged downward by unfamiliarity. Give things a second and third chance before deciding you dislike them.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Advertising Repetition
A consumer products company runs the same television advertisement hundreds of times. The ad itself is not particularly creative or informative. But after seeing it 30 times, something shifts. When you walk down the supermarket aisle and see that brand on the shelf next to an unfamiliar competitor, the familiar brand feels safer, friendlier, and better — even if the quality and price are identical. The company did not improve its product. It just made its name feel familiar. That feeling moves millions of dollars in sales.
Example 2 — The New Neighbor You Eventually Like
A new neighbor moves in. The first time you see them, you feel indifferent — or maybe slightly wary, because you don't know them. But over the following weeks you see them at the mailbox, in the parking lot, at the corner shop. You exchange small waves. Nothing dramatic happens. No deep conversations. But after a month, you realize you like them. They feel like part of your world. Nothing about them changed — only the number of times you were exposed to their face and presence.
Example 3 — Unfamiliar Food You Grow to Love
A child refuses to try a new vegetable — it looks strange and smells different. Their parent puts a small portion on the plate every night for two weeks without forcing them to eat it. By the third week, curiosity takes over and the child tries a bite. By the fourth week, they ask for seconds. Nutritionists call this the "repeated exposure" method. It works because the brain slowly reclassifies a previously strange thing as safe and familiar — and familiarity creates preference almost automatically.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Give new things more chances. Before deciding you dislike a food, person, idea, or place, expose yourself to it several times over a few weeks. Your first reaction is partly just unfamiliarity. Let the mere exposure effect work in favor of a fair assessment.

Step 2 — Recognize it in marketing. When you feel drawn to a brand, product, or candidate, ask whether you like it because it is genuinely good — or because you have simply seen it many times. Familiarity and quality are not the same thing. Checking this protects you from letting repetition substitute for real judgment.

Step 3 — Use it deliberately to build habits. If you want to enjoy exercising, studying, or practicing a skill, show up repeatedly even when it feels neutral or boring. The positive feelings will develop over time through exposure alone. You don't need to love it on day one — you just need to keep showing up.
