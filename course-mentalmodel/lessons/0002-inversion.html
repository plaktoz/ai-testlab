Inversion

Solve problems by thinking backwards — ask what causes failure, then avoid it.

The Big Idea

Most people ask: "How do I make this work?" But smart thinkers ask a different question: "How could this go terribly wrong?"

Then they work backwards — finding every trap and dodging them. What's left is the path to success.

Inversion = Don't just ask how to win.
Ask what would make you lose — then make sure that never happens.

The word "invert" means to flip something upside down. Instead of charging toward a goal, you turn around and look at all the ways to fail. Then you walk away from those failure traps.

The Opposite: Forward-Only Thinking

Most people only think forwards: "I want to be healthy, so I'll exercise more." They focus all their energy on positive steps. They forget to ask: "What habits are secretly making me unwell right now?"

Forward-only thinking misses hidden dangers. Inversion catches them before they can sink you.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Charlie Munger's Secret
Charlie Munger — one of the world's greatest investors — was once asked how to live a good life. He replied: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there." Instead of asking "what makes a great life?" he asked "what destroys a good life?" — then avoided those things.
Source: Farnam Street — Inversion
Example 2 — Staying Healthy
You want to be healthy but every diet fails. Instead of asking "how do I eat better?", ask: "Why do people always give up on eating healthy?" You find the real answers: boring food, no easy snacks, social pressure at restaurants. Fix those failure causes first — and eating well becomes much easier.
Example 3 — Being a Good Friend
You want to be a better friend. Instead of asking "what makes a great friend?", ask: "What do people hate in a bad friend?" Flaking on plans. Only talking about yourself. Never listening. Avoid those things — and you automatically become a much better friend.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Name your goal. Write down what you want to achieve in one simple sentence.

Step 2 — Flip it. Ask: "What are all the ways this could fail completely?" List every failure you can think of.

Step 3 — Block each failure. For each failure on your list, decide: how do I make sure that never happens?
