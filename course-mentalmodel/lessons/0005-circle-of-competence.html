Circle of Competence

Know what you know — and what you don't.

The Big Idea

Picture a circle drawn on a piece of paper. Inside that circle is everything you truly understand — not just know a little about, but deeply get. Outside the circle is everything else. Most people never bother to draw this circle. That's a mistake.

Circle of Competence = The set of things you genuinely understand at a deep level.
The key is knowing exactly where your circle ends — because that's where danger begins.

You can expand your circle over time through study and experience. But first, you must be honest about how big it currently is. Most people think their circle is larger than it really is — and they pay for it.

The Opposite: Overconfidence Outside Your Circle

This is when you act as if you understand something you don't. A doctor gives confident financial advice. A tech entrepreneur launches a restaurant without knowing food service. A friend who read one article about investing tells you exactly which stocks to buy.

The danger isn't ignorance — it's not knowing you're ignorant. When you don't know that you're outside your circle, you make big decisions with false confidence, and mistakes follow.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Warren Buffett's Rule
Warren Buffett, one of the most successful investors of all time, is famous for saying: "I don't invest in things I don't understand." During the dot-com boom of the late 1990s, he refused to buy technology stocks because he didn't understand how those businesses created value. Everyone laughed at him — until the tech bubble burst in 2000 and billions were wiped out. Buffett's portfolio was fine. His circle kept him safe.

Example 2 — The Confident Advice-Giver
Your coworker read a single article about cryptocurrency and is now loudly advising everyone at the office to put their savings into one specific coin. They sound confident. But confidence and competence are not the same thing. Listening to someone outside their circle of competence — and acting on their advice — is one of the most common ways people lose money.
Example 3 — Knowing When to Call an Expert
You notice a strange noise coming from your car's brakes. You know how to drive a car — that's inside your circle. But brake mechanics? That's outside it. Recognizing where your circle ends means you know to call a mechanic instead of guessing. That one act of self-awareness might save your life.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Draw your circle honestly. Write down the areas where you have real, deep knowledge — not just surface-level reading. Be strict with yourself.

Step 2 — Identify the edge. Before making a decision, ask: "Am I inside or outside my circle right now?" If you're outside, find someone whose circle includes this area.

Step 3 — Expand it slowly. You can grow your circle through reading, doing, and learning from experts. But be patient — real competence takes time to build.
