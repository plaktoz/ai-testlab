Confirmation Bias

We see what confirms what we already believe

The Big Idea

Imagine you decide your new coworker is lazy. From that moment on, every time they move slowly, you think: "See? Lazy." But every time they work hard and finish something early, you barely notice. Your brain is quietly running a filter — only letting in the evidence that matches your original verdict.

Confirmation Bias = Your brain secretly collects evidence that supports what you already think — and ignores evidence that challenges it.
It's not lying on purpose. It's just how the brain saves energy: once it has an answer, it stops looking for better ones.

This is one of the most powerful biases humans have. It affects scientists, doctors, judges, and everyone in between. The world is full of information — and our brain acts like a bouncer at a club, only letting in the guests it already likes.

The Trap: Seeking Comfort, Not Truth

The trap shows up every day. You search online for "does coffee cause cancer?" and click only the articles that say no. You share news stories that prove your political team is right. You ask your friend who already agrees with you for advice — and ignore the friend who might push back.

It feels good to be confirmed. Your brain releases a little reward signal when it finds agreement. So seeking confirmation is comfortable, even when it's leading you toward bad decisions. The danger is that you become more and more certain about things that may simply not be true.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Coffee Study Experiment
Researchers gave people studies about coffee — some showed it was good for health, some showed it was bad. People who loved coffee accepted the positive studies quickly and demanded much more proof from the negative ones. People who disliked coffee did the opposite. Same evidence, totally different standards. This was documented across multiple studies on motivated reasoning. (Farnam Street)
Example 2 — The Job Interview
A hiring manager meets a candidate and within the first minute gets a "good feeling." For the rest of the interview, she only asks questions that let the candidate shine. She doesn't probe the weak spots on the resume. By the end, she's certain this is the right hire — but she built that certainty by avoiding the hard questions.
Example 3 — Following Sports News
A fan of a sports team reads every article after a win with excitement. After a loss, they skim past the sports page entirely or only read the ones that blame bad luck. Over time they genuinely believe their team is better than the stats show — because they've been filtering the evidence for years.

How To Spot It (3 Signs)

Sign 1 — You only search for "yes" answers. When you have a question, notice if you're only looking for confirmation. Try typing the opposite into a search engine — "reasons this idea is wrong" — and see what comes up.

Sign 2 — You dismiss opposing evidence too fast. If you hear a fact that disagrees with your view and your first reaction is "that study is probably wrong" or "they're biased," slow down. Ask yourself: would I be this skeptical if this same study supported my view?

Sign 3 — Everyone you ask already agrees with you. If all your advisors have the same opinion as you, you haven't really tested your idea. Deliberately find someone smart who disagrees and ask them to make their best case.
