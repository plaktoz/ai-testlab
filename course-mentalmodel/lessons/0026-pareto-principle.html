Pareto Principle (80/20)

80% of results come from 20% of causes

The Big Idea

You spend your Monday doing 12 different tasks. At the end of the day, two of them — the big presentation you finally finished and the one key email you sent — drove nearly all the real results. The other ten tasks kept you busy, but they barely moved the needle. You felt productive all day. But if you're honest, you know which two tasks actually mattered.

Pareto Principle = Roughly 80% of results come from 20% of causes.
A small number of vital inputs drive most of the outputs. The rest — the "trivial many" — contribute far less than they feel like they do.

Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto first noticed this in 1896 when he found that 20% of Italy's population owned 80% of the land. He then looked at his garden and noticed 20% of his pea pods produced 80% of the peas. The pattern kept appearing everywhere. It's not always exactly 80/20 — it might be 90/10 or 70/30. The principle is: effort and results are rarely distributed evenly.

The Opposite: Treating Everything Equally

The opposite mistake is spreading effort evenly across every task and treating them as if they all matter equally. You make a to-do list of 10 items and chip away at each one by one from top to bottom. You give the same energy to a low-stakes email as to the strategic decision that could change everything.

This feels responsible and thorough. It is actually a trap. When you treat all tasks as equal, low-priority work crowds out high-priority work. You stay busy but not productive. You finish many things that barely mattered and neglect the few things that did.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Business Revenue
Most businesses discover that roughly 20% of their customers generate 80% of their revenue. These are the loyal, high-value clients who buy frequently and don't haggle on price. Smart businesses identify this 20% and invest heavily in serving them well — faster support, personal attention, early access to new products. Meanwhile, they spend far less energy on the 80% of customers who together contribute only 20% of the money.
Example 2 — Software Bugs
Engineering teams at major software companies have consistently found that roughly 20% of bugs are responsible for 80% of the crashes and user complaints. By identifying and fixing that critical 20% first, you eliminate most of the user pain very quickly without touching the other 80% of minor issues. This is why experienced engineers triage rather than squash every bug in strict order of discovery.
Example 3 — Your Wardrobe
Look in your closet. You probably have dozens of items of clothing. But you rotate through maybe 5 or 10 outfits that you actually love and reach for every week. Most of the closet sits untouched for months — maybe years. The same pattern appears in your daily habits (a few drive most of your wellbeing), your relationships (a few matter far more than the many), and your daily schedule (a few hours contain most of your real output).

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — List everything. Make an honest, complete list of all the tasks, customers, habits, or relationships you're considering. Don't filter yet — just get them all down.

Step 2 — Find the vital 20%. Ask: which items on this list produce the majority of real results? Look for the few that punch far above their weight.

Step 3 — Shift your energy. Spend most of your time and attention on the vital few. Cut back on, delegate, or eliminate the trivial many. Protect the 20% ruthlessly.
