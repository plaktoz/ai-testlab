Incentives

Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome

The Big Idea

Why does your mechanic sometimes recommend repairs you don't need? Why did Wells Fargo employees open millions of fake bank accounts? Why does a doctor paid per procedure seem to find more things that need fixing? The answer isn't that all these people are bad. It's that they were all responding to incentives.

Incentives = People do what they are rewarded for doing.
Before asking "why does this happen?", ask: "What reward is making this behaviour happen?"

The investor Charlie Munger put it plainly: "Show me the incentive and I'll show you the outcome." This is one of the most powerful ideas in understanding human behaviour — and in designing systems that work. When the incentive changes, the behaviour changes. It's almost mechanical.

The Opposite: Blaming People, Not Systems

Our natural reaction when someone behaves badly is to blame their character: they're greedy, lazy, dishonest. But most bad behaviour comes from bad incentive systems, not bad people. Put almost anyone into a system that rewards the wrong things, and they will do the wrong things. Put good people into a broken incentive structure and the structure usually wins.

This matters enormously when you want to fix a problem. If you blame the person, you fire them and hire someone else who will do the same thing. If you blame the incentive, you fix the system and the behaviour changes automatically. Smart leaders redesign incentives. Everyone else just keeps being disappointed by people.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Wells Fargo Fake Accounts Scandal
Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees opened approximately 3.5 million fake bank and credit card accounts — without customers' knowledge. Why did thousands of employees do this? Because they were set aggressive sales targets. Every new account opened earned a reward. Hit your targets or face consequences. The employees weren't all secretly corrupt. They were responding to exactly the incentive they were given. When the incentive was discovered and removed, the fake accounts stopped. The bank paid $3 billion in fines — but the real lesson is about what incentives produce.

Example 2 — The Doctor Paid Per Procedure
In a healthcare system that pays doctors for each test, scan, and procedure they order, doctors tend to order more of them. This isn't because all those doctors are dishonest. It's because the system rewards action over restraint. Studies comparing fee-for-service systems to salaried or outcome-based systems find consistently that fee-for-service produces more interventions — regardless of medical need. Change the incentive from "paid per procedure" to "paid for patient outcomes" and the same doctors recommend fewer, better-targeted procedures. Same people. Different incentive. Different behaviour.
Example 3 — The Sales Team Paid Only on Volume
A company pays its salespeople purely on how many deals they close, with no measure of whether those customers stay or are happy. Predictably, the sales team closes as many deals as possible — including selling to customers who are a poor fit. Those customers churn quickly, complain loudly, and cost the support team enormous time. The company blames the salespeople for "overselling." But the real culprit is the commission structure. Add a measure for customer retention and the salespeople immediately start qualifying leads more carefully. Incentives designed the problem. Redesigned incentives solved it.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Find the incentive behind the behaviour. When something puzzling or frustrating happens, ask: "What is this person or group being rewarded for?" Follow the reward — you'll find the behaviour attached to it.

Step 2 — Check your own incentives. Ask honestly: "What am I being rewarded for right now? Does that align with what I actually want to achieve?" Many personal failures come from accidentally incentivising the wrong things in yourself.

Step 3 — Redesign, don't blame. When you want to change behaviour — in a team, a company, or yourself — change the incentive structure. Praise, punishment, money, and recognition are all powerful levers. Aim them at the right target.
