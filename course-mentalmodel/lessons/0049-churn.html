Churn

Constant loss that must be offset by new growth just to stay in place

The Big Idea

Imagine filling a bucket with water. You pour steadily. But the bucket has a hole in the bottom. You pour in one cup — half leaks out. You pour in two — one leaks out. You work hard just to stay at the same level. Pour in less than you lose, and the bucket empties no matter how hard you try. That hole is churn.

Churn = the rate at which customers, employees, or members leave a system over time.
If you don't replace what's lost, you shrink — even when you feel like you're growing.

Every business, team, and relationship has some churn. Customers cancel subscriptions. Staff resign. Friends drift apart. A healthy system tracks this loss honestly. A 5% annual churn rate means you must replace one in twenty people just to stay flat. A 20% rate means you replace one in five every year — and if you don't attract replacements, you are shrinking while thinking you're standing still. The lesson: before celebrating new growth, check the size of the hole in your bucket.

The Trap: Celebrating New Arrivals While Ignoring Departures

Companies obsess over new customer acquisition. Marketing budgets are enormous. Sign-up campaigns are celebrated. But if nobody asks how many customers left last month, the numbers can look rosy while the business is quietly dying. A company that signs 100 new customers each month but loses 95 is technically growing by 5 — but it has a churn crisis that will eventually overwhelm the acquisition effort.

The same applies to teams. If the best people keep leaving, the team never builds cumulative talent. It trains new people endlessly while the average skill level stays low. Fix the hole first. Then pour water.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Gym with the Leaky Bucket
A gym chain celebrates signing up 900 new members each year. The sales team gets bonuses. The marketing campaign is declared a success. Nobody mentions that 800 members cancel each year. Net growth: 100 members per year, on a base of several thousand — barely keeping pace with anything. Worse, the gym is spending enormous money on acquisition for almost no net gain. A competitor gym that keeps 95% of its members every year is growing powerfully without adding a single new one. Low churn is more powerful than high acquisition.
Example 2 — The Team That Never Accumulates Talent
A growing tech company hires aggressively. The team doubles every eighteen months. But the most talented engineers leave after a year, frustrated by management and poor culture. The company is always training new people. The institutional knowledge walks out with the departing staff. Products ship late. Quality stays low. The team has been large for years, but never feels experienced. High churn in people means high churn in knowledge, trust, and momentum. The solution wasn't hiring more — it was making the company worth staying in.
Example 3 — The Newsletter That Keeps Growing (or Isn't)
A writer publishes a newsletter with 10,000 subscribers. Each month, 200 new people subscribe — but 180 unsubscribe. The writer thinks they are growing steadily and celebrates each new 200. But net growth is only 20 per month: at that rate, they need five years to double in size. A writer who publishes content that people actually stay for — with only 50 unsubscribes per month — grows six times faster with the same acquisition effort. In every membership system, the growth rate is acquisition minus churn. Track both numbers, always.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Measure the hole. Find out what your actual churn rate is — in customers, subscribers, employees, or whatever matters most in your system. If you've never measured it, you don't know if you're growing or shrinking in real terms.

Step 2 — Find why people leave. Talk to those who left. Look for patterns. Is it price? Product? Culture? Service? The exit interview or cancellation survey is one of the most valuable data sources you have, and most organisations ignore it completely.

Step 3 — Fix the hole before scaling acquisition. Adding more water to a leaky bucket before fixing the hole wastes effort and money. Reducing churn by even a few percentage points can be worth more than doubling your acquisition — because the improvement compounds every month.
