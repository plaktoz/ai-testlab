Activation Energy

Every change needs an initial push to get started

The Big Idea

You bought the gym shoes six months ago. They sit by the wardrobe. You want to exercise — you really do. But the bag is in the cupboard, the shoes need tying, the gym is a ten-minute drive, and it's cold outside. None of those things are hard. Together, they feel like a mountain. So you don't go. Again.

Activation Energy = the minimum push required to start a reaction.
Lower it for good habits. Raise it for bad ones.

In chemistry, activation energy is the spark needed to start a chemical reaction. In life, it's every small obstacle that stands between you and starting something. James Clear, who wrote Atomic Habits, put it perfectly: put your gym bag by the front door the night before. Now the activation energy is almost zero. Want to stop doomscrolling? Put your phone in another room. Now picking it up costs effort. The behaviour didn't change — only how easy or hard it is to start.

The Trap: Making Good Things Hard and Bad Things Easy

Most people design their environment by accident. The junk food is on the kitchen counter. The healthy food is in the back of the fridge. The TV remote is always in reach. The book is upstairs. Accidentally, they've made bad habits easy and good habits hard.

The fix isn't willpower. Willpower runs out. The fix is environment design. Make the good choice the path of least resistance. Make the bad choice require effort. Your future self will almost always take the easy path — so make the easy path the right one.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Gym Bag by the Door
A person who wants to exercise in the morning sets out their gym clothes, shoes, and water bottle the night before. They sleep in their gym clothes if necessary. In the morning, every step toward the gym is already done. The activation energy has been cut to almost nothing — so they go. The same person who kept their bag in the cupboard missed every workout. The habit didn't change. The friction did.
Example 2 — The Meeting That Never Gets Called
A team has a persistent problem. Everyone agrees they need to sit down and fix it. But nobody calls the meeting. Nobody books the room. Nobody sends the invite. Weeks pass. The problem stays. The activation energy for scheduling the meeting — finding a time, booking space, writing an agenda — was just high enough that no one bothered. One person who books it immediately finds the meeting happens the next day and the problem is solved in an hour. Start the thing. Lower the energy needed to begin.
Example 3 — Healthy Food Within Reach
A nutritionist once said: "Put a bowl of fruit on your counter and hide the biscuits at the back of a high shelf." It sounds too simple. But it works. When you're hungry and in a hurry, you grab whatever is nearest. If fruit is closest, you eat fruit. If biscuits are closest, you eat biscuits. The food is the same. Your hunger is the same. Only the activation energy is different. Good choices became effortless. Bad choices became a small obstacle.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Identify the friction. Pick a habit you want to build or break. List every small obstacle that stands between you and doing it (or not doing it). Even one-step obstacles count.

Step 2 — Remove steps for good habits. Pre-load everything you need. Put it in plain sight. Make starting require zero decisions. The goal is to make beginning feel automatic.

Step 3 — Add steps for bad habits. Put distance and effort between you and the thing you want to avoid. Log out of social media apps. Keep snacks out of the house. Make the bad choice cost at least one extra deliberate action.
