Opportunity Cost

Choosing one thing means giving up the next best thing

The Big Idea

You spend all Saturday watching TV. Nothing went wrong. You didn't lose any money. But something very real was lost — you just can't see it on a receipt. That day could have been a long run, a side project, or time with someone you love. The cost of watching TV wasn't the electricity bill. It was everything else you could have done instead.

Opportunity Cost = The value of the best thing you gave up by making a choice.
Every "yes" hides a silent "no" to something else — and that hidden "no" has a real cost.

You can't see opportunity cost on a price tag. It doesn't show up in your bank statement. That's what makes it so easy to ignore — and so important to think about. Every resource you have — money, time, attention — can only be used once. Spend it here, and it's gone from everywhere else.

The Opposite: Ignoring What You're Giving Up

Most people only count the visible cost of a decision: the ticket price, the fee, the time the meeting takes. They forget the invisible cost — what they could have done with that same time or money instead. A doctor might judge a $1,000 holiday by asking "can I afford this?" instead of "is this the best use of $1,000?"

Ignoring opportunity cost leads to bad trades. You accept a slightly better job offer while passing on a much better one you didn't bother exploring. You spend the evening on low-priority emails and miss the deep work that would have moved your biggest project forward. The invisible alternative is always there — whether you count it or not.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — Saturday on the Sofa
You spend all day Saturday watching a TV series. The series was free on a subscription you already pay for. So it cost you nothing — right? Wrong. You gave up a full day. In that day you could have exercised, worked on your side business, called a friend, or learned a skill. The opportunity cost of that Saturday wasn't money. It was everything on that list. Most of the time we weigh what we're doing against "doing nothing" — but doing nothing is never really an option. There's always a next best use.

Example 2 — The Real Cost of University
Going to university for four years costs tuition — let's say $40,000 in fees. But that's only half the real cost. During those four years, you could have been working and earning money. If you could have earned $30,000 a year, that's $120,000 in lost income — on top of the tuition. The true cost of a four-year degree is often closer to $160,000 once you count the opportunity cost. This doesn't mean university is a bad decision. It just means you should account for the full price before choosing.
Example 3 — Warren Buffett's Key Question
When Warren Buffett considers buying a company, he doesn't just ask: "Is this a good deal?" He asks: "Is this the best possible thing I can do with this money right now?" Because saying yes to one investment means saying no to every other investment he could make instead. Buffett keeps a mental list of the best alternatives. Every new opportunity gets compared to that list. Only if it beats the best alternative does he say yes. That's opportunity cost thinking at the highest level.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Name the best alternative. Before any big decision, ask: "What is the single best other thing I could do with this time or money?" Write it down. Make it concrete. That's your opportunity cost.

Step 2 — Compare directly. Put the option on the table next to its best alternative and compare them honestly. Don't compare it to "nothing." Nothing is never the real alternative.

Step 3 — Include your time. Time is the resource most people forget to price. An hour spent on a low-value task isn't free — it's an hour taken from your highest-value task. Apply the same test to your calendar that you apply to your wallet.
