Occam's Razor

The simplest explanation is usually right.

The Big Idea

Your dog knocked over the trash can last night. Could it be a burglar who carefully replaced everything except the trash? Or could it just be your dog? Occam's Razor says: go with the simpler answer first.

Occam's Razor = When two explanations fit the facts, the simpler one is more likely true.
Don't pile on extra assumptions when a plain explanation already works.

Named after William of Ockham, a 14th-century English philosopher, this principle is sometimes stated as: "Entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity." In plain English: don't make things more complicated than they need to be.

The Opposite: Overcomplication

Overcomplication is when we invent complex stories to explain simple events. Your friend didn't text back — so you spend an hour building theories about why they might be angry, busy, or hurt. The real reason? Their phone was on silent.

Complex explanations feel more intelligent. But they're usually wrong. Adding unnecessary steps wastes time and leads you away from the real answer.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Car That Won't Start
You turn the key and your car makes a clicking sound but doesn't start. You could imagine a cracked fuel injector, a failed starter motor, a damaged alternator — or a dozen other faults. But Occam's Razor says: check the battery first. A dead battery is the simplest explanation that fits the evidence — and it's right the vast majority of the time. Mechanics are trained to rule out simple causes before opening up the engine.

Example 2 — The Doctor's Diagnosis
A patient walks in with a runny nose, a mild cough, and a low fever. Those symptoms could theoretically point to a rare tropical disease — but they also perfectly describe a common cold. Occam's Razor guides doctors to treat for the common cold first. There's even a medical saying: "When you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras." Simple, common explanations should be tested before rare, exotic ones.
Example 3 — The Missing Keys
You can't find your house keys. You could wonder if someone moved them, if they fell into the couch, if your bag has a hidden hole — or you can simply think back to where you last had them. Nine times out of ten, they're in the last coat you wore or right by the door. The simplest explanation — you set them down in a usual spot — is almost always right.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — List your explanations. Write down every possible reason something happened, from the simplest to the wildest.

Step 2 — Count the assumptions. Look at each explanation and count how many extra assumptions it needs to be true. The one with the fewest is usually the winner.

Step 3 — Test the simplest first. Start with the simplest explanation. Only move to complex ones if the simple one is clearly ruled out.
