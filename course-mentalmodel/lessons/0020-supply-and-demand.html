Supply and Demand

Scarcity and desire set prices — and your value

The Big Idea

Your pipe bursts at midnight on Christmas Eve. Water is everywhere. You call ten plumbers and only one answers. That plumber quotes you three times the normal rate. Are they a villain? Or are they responding to a simple law? When something is rare and people desperately need it, the price goes up. That's supply and demand.

Supply and Demand = When something is scarce AND people want it, its price rises.
When something is abundant AND few people want it, the price falls.

Supply is how much of something exists. Demand is how much people want it. These two forces push and pull against each other until they reach a price where buyers and sellers agree. This applies to bread, houses, concert tickets — and to your own skills and time. Understanding this makes the economy, prices, and even salaries feel less like magic and more like logic.

The Opposite: Ignoring Scarcity and Demand

Many people are surprised when prices rise suddenly. They feel it's unfair or random. But prices almost always move for a reason: supply shrank, demand grew, or both happened at once. Ignoring this means being repeatedly blindsided — by rent rises, by salary stagnation, by industries that collapse.

The same blind spot can cost you in your career. Learning a skill when it's rare and in demand makes you very valuable. Staying in a field that is being automated away — where supply of workers is high and demand is falling — means your value keeps shrinking. Supply and demand doesn't judge. It just calculates.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Emergency Plumber
On a normal Tuesday, a plumber charges $80 per hour. There are twelve plumbers available in your area. After a big storm floods half the city, all twelve plumbers are booked solid for a week. The one plumber who has a cancellation tomorrow can now charge $250 per hour — and people will pay it. Nothing changed about the plumber's skill. What changed is that supply dropped (fewer available) while demand spiked (everyone needs help now). Scarcity plus urgency equals higher price. The law worked exactly as expected.
Source: Farnam Street — Mental Models
Example 2 — The Coder Who Learned Early
In 2010, the world needed software but very few people knew how to build it. Someone who learned to code in 2008 walked into a job market with high demand and tiny supply. The result: massive salaries, rapid promotions, your pick of companies. By 2025, tens of millions of people had learned to code. Supply grew enormously. Good coders are still well paid — but the extreme scarcity premium is gone. The lesson: getting to a high-demand, low-supply skill early compounds your advantage dramatically.
Example 3 — Concert Ticket Resale Prices
A hugely popular band announces a concert. The arena holds 20,000 seats. Fifty million fans want to go. The tickets sell out in four minutes. On the resale market, a $120 face-value ticket now sells for $600 or $700. No one changed the seats. No one changed the performance. What changed is the gap between supply (20,000 seats) and demand (millions of fans). The resale market is just supply and demand working without a speed limit. When supply is fixed and demand is enormous, price climbs until demand cools.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Ask what's scarce. When a price surprises you — high or low — ask: is supply limited? Has demand changed? Usually one of those two things explains the price. Train yourself to look for the force behind the number.

Step 2 — Apply it to your own value. Your salary is a price — set by supply and demand for your skills. Ask: how many people can do exactly what I do? How urgently do companies need it? A rare skill in high demand is the most reliable path to being well paid.

Step 3 — Look around corners. Supply and demand changes before prices always follow. Watch for new technologies, shifting populations, and changing industries. The person who spots a coming shortage in supply — or a surge in demand — before others do can position themselves ahead of the price move.
