# 48 of 50

Redundancy

Backup systems prevent small failures from becoming catastrophic

The Big Idea

You are driving to the most important job interview of your career. It is two hours from home. You have no spare tyre. Halfway there, you get a puncture. Interview missed. Opportunity gone. A spare tyre costs almost nothing compared to what just happened. That spare tyre is redundancy — a backup that sits idle until the day it saves you.

Redundancy = having backup systems so that one failure doesn't collapse the whole thing.
Redundancy looks wasteful — until the day it saves everything.

Engineering and aviation learned this lesson in blood. Modern passenger aircraft have three or four independent backup systems for every critical function. If the main hydraulic system fails, a second kicks in. If that fails, there's a third. The plane does not fall from the sky because of one failure. The human body is full of redundancy too: two kidneys, two lungs, two eyes. You can lose one of each and continue to live. Nature figured out redundancy millions of years before engineers did.

The Trap: Cutting Backup Systems to Save Money

In normal times, redundancy looks like waste. The spare tyre just sits there. The emergency fund earns low interest. The backup employee is "unnecessary overhead." The second supplier is "an extra cost." So businesses cut them. Households spend their buffer. Countries eliminate strategic reserves. Everything runs perfectly — until the day it doesn't. Then a single point of failure triggers a cascade that could have been stopped by a backup that cost a fraction of the damage.

The lesson is counterintuitive: the strength of a system is often measured not by how well it performs when everything is fine, but by how little damage it takes when one piece breaks.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Emergency Fund
Financial advisors recommend keeping three to six months of living expenses in a savings account that you never touch. To a young person building their life, this feels like money sitting uselessly in a bank account. Then the car breaks down, the boiler needs replacing, and they lose their job — all in the same six-month period. The person with the emergency fund covers every crisis without going into debt. Life continues. The person without it borrows at high interest, adds financial stress to every other problem, and takes years to recover. The emergency fund is financial redundancy. It only reveals its value in a crisis.
Example 2 — The 80% Client Problem
A freelance designer lands a dream client who accounts for 80% of their income. Life is great. Work is steady. Money is reliable. Then the client is acquired by a competitor, the team is restructured, and the designer's contract is cancelled in a week. With 80% of income gone, the designer has no redundancy in their client base. A designer who had spread work across five or six clients of similar size would have lost 15–20% of their income at worst — painful, but survivable. Never let one source carry most of your critical weight.
Example 3 — Cross-Training Employees
A small bakery has one person who knows how to operate the main oven system. Everyone else has never been trained on it. One morning, that person is sick. The bakery cannot open. Deliveries are missed. Regular customers go elsewhere. A bakery where two or three people know how to run every critical function never has this problem. Cross-training is human redundancy. It costs time and money to train backup people — but the cost of not having them arrives all at once, on the worst possible day.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Find your single points of failure. Ask: what happens if this one thing fails — this person, this income source, this system, this supplier? If the answer is "everything stops," you have found a dangerous gap that redundancy can fix.

Step 2 — Add at least one backup. You don't need infinite backups — just enough that one failure doesn't bring down the system. A second client. A second income source. A second team member trained on critical tasks. A savings buffer. One backup changes fragile into resilient.

Step 3 — Test your backups before you need them. A spare tyre you've never inflated may be flat when you need it. Test backup systems regularly. Check your emergency fund is accessible. Ensure the backup employee has actually been trained, not just told they're the backup.
