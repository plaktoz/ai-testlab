Game Theory

Your best move depends on what others will do

The Big Idea

Imagine two kids both spot a $10 bill on the ground at the same time. If they share nicely, each gets $5. If one grabs it all, they keep $10 while the other gets zero. If both grab at once, they rip it and both get nothing. What should you do? It depends entirely on what the other kid is going to do. That's the heart of game theory.

Game Theory = The study of how people make decisions when the best choice depends on what others choose.
Before you move, you have to think about their move — and what they think your move will be.

Economists, military strategists, and business leaders use game theory to think through negotiations, price wars, and arms races. One famous result is the Nash Equilibrium: the point where no single player can do better by changing only their own move. It's not always the best outcome for everyone — it's just the stable one.

The Opposite: Acting as if No One Responds

Most people plan as if the world will hold still. They choose the option that looks best for them — without asking how others will react. A company lowers its price, expecting to win customers. But the rival lowers their price too. Now both companies make less money and neither gained market share.

Ignoring the game turns clever-looking plans into costly mistakes. Every time you act, the other players adjust. That's not a surprise — it's the whole game.

Real-Life Examples

Example 1 — The Price War
Two coffee shops sit across the street from each other. One drops its latte price by 50 cents. The other matches it immediately. Then the first drops again. Within a month, both are selling below cost. Neither gained customers — they just both started losing money. This is a classic game theory trap: each player's individual best move leads to a result that hurts everyone.
Example 2 — The 4-Way Stop
You pull up to a 4-way stop at the same moment as another car. You're both deciding: do I go, or do I wait? You make eye contact. You each hesitate. You each gesture. Eventually someone goes first. This tiny moment is a real-time game: both players are reading signals, predicting the other's move, and adjusting in real time. It works because both sides share a rule about who arrived first.
Example 3 — The Arms Treaty
Two countries both want to feel safe. Neither wants to spend money on weapons. But if one builds a bigger army, the other feels threatened and builds a larger one. Both end up spending billions on weapons neither wants to use. This is the Nash Equilibrium of the arms race — each country's best individual response leads to an outcome that's bad for both. Treaties only work if both sides can trust the other to hold.

How To Use It (3 Steps)

Step 1 — Map the players. Who else has a stake in this decision? List every person or group who will react to what you do.

Step 2 — Think from their side. What is their best move? What are they afraid of losing? What do they gain if you move first?

Step 3 — Find the stable point. Is there an outcome where neither side has a reason to change? That's the equilibrium — and it's where things tend to settle.
