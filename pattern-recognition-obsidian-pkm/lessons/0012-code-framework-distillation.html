CODE Framework — Distillation as Pattern Compression

~20 min Module 4 — Daily Workflow
Primary Source
Forte Labs — Building a Second Brain Overview

Building a Second Brain (BASB) is a system for creating a digital repository of knowledge — organized not by subject but by actionability — so that your biological brain is freed from storage and reserved for having ideas. Its four-step CODE framework structures how information flows from capture through expression, with Distillation (Progressive Summarization) serving as the mechanism that compresses raw notes into reusable, pattern-revealing packets.

The CODE Framework

The premise of Building a Second Brain is straightforward but consequential: brains are for having ideas, not storing them. The overhead of remembering where you filed something, what you read three months ago, or which project a particular insight belongs to consumes cognitive capacity that could otherwise be spent thinking. A second brain — a digital repository you maintain deliberately — takes over the storage function and returns that capacity to you.

Forte Labs structures this system around four steps, collectively called CODE:

  • Capture — Save what resonates. When you encounter information that seems useful, interesting, or relevant, get it into the system. The threshold for capturing is low: if it sparked any reaction in you, it belongs in the vault.
  • Organize — Sort by actionability, not by subject. Rather than filing notes into topical categories, the BASB approach asks what project or goal each note serves. Information that has a near-term use is more accessible than information filed into a subject archive that may never be revisited.
  • Distill — Compress notes using Progressive Summarization, a layering technique in which you add highlights at increasing levels of abstraction so a note can be reviewed at varying depths without re-reading it in full.
  • Express — Convert stored knowledge into output using Intermediate Packets: discrete, reusable work units built from distilled notes, which can be assembled into larger deliverables rather than created from scratch each time.

Each step in CODE addresses a different failure mode. Capture prevents good ideas from slipping away. Organize prevents the archive from becoming a search problem. Distill prevents notes from being too long to use under pressure. Express prevents the system from remaining permanently in input mode — notes that are never acted upon are just storage, not a second brain.

Progressive Summarization — Distillation in Practice

Of the four CODE steps, Distill is the one most directly relevant to pattern recognition. Progressive Summarization is a layered compression technique: you do not rewrite a note or reduce it to a single sentence on first pass. Instead, you add layers over time, each more selective than the last.

The mechanics are simple. On the first pass, read the note and bold every sentence or phrase that seems important — aim for roughly 20% of the total text. You are not summarizing yet; you are marking. On the second pass, read only the bolded text and highlight the most important 20% of what you bolded. These highlighted passages are the loadbearing ideas — the statements that would survive even the most aggressive compression. A third pass, if warranted, produces a 2-sentence summary at the top of the note using only what the highlights already contain, with no new ideas introduced.

What makes this a pattern recognition exercise is the cognitive demand it places on you during each pass. To bold the right 20%, you have to form a judgment about what matters most in this material — which implies a working model of relevance. To highlight 20% of the bolded text, you have to make an even sharper judgment about which ideas are truly load-bearing versus merely interesting. That act of repeated compression forces you to identify the underlying structure of the note: what is it really about, at its core?

As Forte Labs describes it, Progressive Summarization allows notes to be reviewed at varying depths. A reader in a hurry reads only the highlights. A reader with more time reads the bolded passages. A reader who needs the full context reads the original. The note now serves multiple review speeds without requiring multiple notes — the structure carries the compression.

How Connections Surface Through Accumulation

The distillation process does not only make individual notes more usable. Over time, as notes accumulate in the vault, something structural begins to happen: cross-domain connections surface organically. The second brain starts to act as a mirror that reveals underlying structures in your thinking and your goals.

This is not a designed feature of any single note — it is an emergent property of the collection. When you have distilled dozens of notes across different domains and topics, the highlights and summaries you have extracted begin to echo one another. The same core idea reappears in a business article, a psychology paper, and a historical biography. A principle you distilled from one field turns out to be a restatement of a principle you distilled from another. These repetitions are pattern signals: the structure is appearing in multiple contexts, which is the definition of a pattern worth encoding.

The key phrase from Forte Labs is that this mirror reveals underlying structures in your thinking and your goals — not just in the source material. The notes you chose to capture, the phrases you chose to bold, and the sentences you chose to highlight are all selections made by you according to your interests and priorities. The accumulated pattern of those selections is a map of what you find significant. When cross-domain connections surface, they are telling you something about the structure of your own intellectual attention, not just about the topics themselves.

Intermediate Packets — the Express step's output — are built from this distilled material. Because each packet is discrete and reusable, you can assemble new work from existing compressed knowledge rather than starting from scratch. The packet might be a refined argument, a structured summary, a set of examples, or a diagram. Whatever form it takes, it is built from distillation, not from raw notes — and that distinction is what makes the second brain a system for thinking rather than a system for filing.

Key Insight

The Distill step of CODE is a practical daily mechanism for forcing pattern compression. The act of re-summarizing your own notes at increasing levels of abstraction — bolding the most important 20%, then highlighting the most important 20% of those — is a pattern recognition exercise in itself. Over time, as distilled notes accumulate, cross-domain connections surface organically, acting as a mirror that reveals underlying structures in your thinking and goals.

Common Misconception

Progressive Summarization is not meant to be applied to every note you capture. Forte Labs is explicit that the system should be lightweight: only return to distill a note when you find yourself using it again. Distilling a one-time capture that you will never revisit is wasted effort. The technique pays off on notes you return to repeatedly — those are precisely the notes most likely to contain patterns worth surfacing.

Skill Exercise

Apply Progressive Summarization

Take any note in your vault that is longer than 200 words. Apply Progressive Summarization in three passes. Pass 1: read the full note and bold every sentence or phrase that seems important — aim for 20% of the total text. Pass 2: read only the bolded text and highlight the most important 20% of what you bolded. These are the loadbearing ideas. Pass 3: at the top of the note, write a 2-sentence summary using only what you highlighted — no new ideas, just compression. Then ask yourself: does the 2-sentence summary reveal a pattern or priority you had not consciously noticed before?

How to Do It

  1. Capture: When you encounter useful information — an article, a conversation, an observation — get it into your vault within 24 hours. Do not perfect it; just capture it.
  2. Organize: File it under the relevant PARA category (Project, Area, Resource, or Archive). If you are unsure, use Resource.
  3. Distill — Pass 1: Read the note once. Bold every sentence or phrase that seems important. Aim for 20% of the total text.
  4. Distill — Pass 2: Read only the bolded text. Highlight the most important 20% of what you bolded. These are the loadbearing ideas.
  5. Distill — Pass 3: At the top of the note, write a 2-sentence summary using only what you highlighted. No new ideas — compress what is already there.
  6. Express: Use the note. Write from it, reference it in a meeting, link to it from a project. A note that is never expressed is just storage.
  7. Do not distill every note — only ones you return to more than once. One-time captures do not need Progressive Summarization.
