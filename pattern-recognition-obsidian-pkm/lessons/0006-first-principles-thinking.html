First Principles Thinking — Deriving Patterns Yourself

~20 min Module 2 — Mental Frameworks
Primary Source
Farnam Street — First Principles Thinking

First principles thinking strips a problem down to its most fundamental, irreducible truths and rebuilds understanding from that foundation — contrasting with analogical reasoning, which makes only incremental improvements to models borrowed from others. Because most people stop at borrowed patterns, first principles thinkers uncover structural realities hidden beneath layers of assumption, and the rewards for doing so tend to be non-linear.

What Is First Principles Thinking?

First principles thinking is the practice of breaking a problem apart until you reach its most fundamental, irreducible truths — and then rebuilding your understanding from that foundation. Rather than accepting the existing shape of a problem and refining around its edges, you dissolve the accumulated layers of assumption, convention, and borrowed wisdom until you are left with only what cannot be further reduced. From that bedrock, you reason upward on your own terms.

The alternative — and the path most people take most of the time — is analogical reasoning: taking what already exists, making slight improvements, and treating the current state of the art as the natural starting point. Analogical reasoning is not wrong; it is fast and often good enough. But Farnam Street notes that it keeps you "in the shadow of others." You are iterating on someone else's pattern, which means the ceiling of your insight is set by whoever built the original model.

First principles thinking removes that ceiling. When you derive the underlying structure yourself rather than borrowing it, you produce a pattern that is genuinely yours — one built from the actual physics of the problem rather than from the accumulated habits of the field. This is why first principles is best understood as a pattern-creation technique: its output is not a refined version of an existing pattern but an independently derived structure that may look nothing like what came before.

Socratic Questioning and the Five Whys

Two practical techniques make first principles thinking operational rather than abstract. The first is Socratic questioning — systematically interrogating the assumptions behind any belief or practice by asking what it rests on, whether that foundation can be verified, and whether alternative foundations are possible. Socratic questioning is a method of disciplined skepticism: you are not trying to refute claims but to expose which parts of them are load-bearing and which are inherited convention that has never been seriously examined.

The second technique is the Five Whys: starting from a belief or observation and asking "Why is this true?" repeatedly, writing a one-sentence answer each time, until you can no longer answer without circularity or guesswork. That point of silence is bedrock — the irreducible truth beneath the belief. In practice, four to six iterations is usually sufficient. Each layer you peel away removes an assumption that most practitioners in a field have stopped questioning, which means each layer also reveals a structural reality that most practitioners have stopped seeing.

Both techniques accomplish the same thing: they force you to identify what you actually know versus what you have absorbed by osmosis. The gap between those two categories is where conventional wisdom lives — and conventional wisdom, by definition, is where everyone else has already stopped. First principles thinkers treat that gap as the most interesting place to work.

Why First Principles Produces Non-Linear Rewards

The Farnam Street analysis makes a pointed observation about the payoff structure of first principles thinking: rewards tend to be non-linear. This is not a coincidence. When you identify the gap between what currently exists and what is physically or logically achievable — by reasoning from irreducible truths rather than from the current state of the art — you are operating in territory that analogical thinkers cannot reach. Most of the competitive crowd is clustered around the existing frontier, making marginal improvements. A first principles thinker who reaches bedrock and reasons back up may arrive at a conclusion that the frontier crowd cannot even perceive as possible, because they never questioned the assumption that ruled it out.

The non-linearity also follows from the transferability of the derived pattern. A pattern you borrowed carries the implicit constraints of the domain it came from. A pattern you derived yourself from irreducible truths can be applied anywhere those truths hold — which is typically a much wider set of contexts than the original source domain. First principles patterns are more portable, more robust to domain shifts, and more likely to generate novel implications when applied in new settings. That combination — deeper insight plus broader application — is the structural source of the non-linear reward.

Key Insight

First principles thinking is a pattern-creation technique: rather than borrowing someone else's pattern and iterating on it, you strip the problem to its irreducible truths and derive the underlying structure yourself. Because most people stop at borrowed patterns, first principles thinkers uncover structural realities that the field has stopped questioning — and the rewards for doing so tend to be non-linear, because the derived pattern is both deeper and more transferable than any borrowed alternative.

Common Misconception

First principles thinking is sometimes mistaken for rejecting all prior knowledge or starting every problem from a blank slate. That is not what the technique requires. The point is not to discard what others have learned but to understand why the accepted models are shaped the way they are — to know which parts rest on physical or logical necessity and which parts are inherited convention. Analogical reasoning becomes a liability only when you mistake convention for bedrock. Once you know the actual foundation, you can borrow existing models deliberately, knowing exactly where they are reliable and where they are not.

Skill Exercise

Apply the Five Whys

Choose a common belief or best-practice in your field — something you have accepted and acted on without deeply questioning it. Apply the Five Whys: ask "Why is this true?" and write a one-sentence answer, then ask why that is true, and repeat until you hit bedrock — the point where you can no longer answer without circularity or guesswork, typically four to six levels deep. Write down the irreducible truth you find at that bottom layer, and then identify one new implication that the original belief obscured by assuming that bedrock away.

How to Do It

  1. Write down one belief, best practice, or assumption you hold in your field — something you have accepted without deeply questioning.
  2. Ask "Why is this true?" Write the answer in one sentence.
  3. Ask "Why is that true?" Write the answer. Repeat until you can no longer answer — this is bedrock (typically 4–6 levels deep).
  4. At bedrock, write: "The irreducible truth here is: ___." This is your first principle.
  5. Now rebuild upward: given only this first principle, what would you design or conclude? Write 2–3 sentences.
  6. Compare your rebuilt conclusion to the original belief. Write one thing the original belief got right and one thing it obscured or got wrong.
  7. If the new implication is actionable, add it to your task list or project notes immediately.
