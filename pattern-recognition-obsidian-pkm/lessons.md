## Module 1 — Cognitive Foundations of Pattern Recognition
*Establishes the scientific bedrock: how experts build and retrieve patterns, and what specific mechanisms accelerate this. Must come first because every subsequent module depends on understanding what we're training.*

### Lesson 1: Deliberate Practice and Expert Pattern Libraries
**File:** `lessons/0001-deliberate-practice-chunk-recognition.html`
**Key concepts:** deliberate practice · chunk recognition · volume + feedback loop
**Source paper:** https://jamesclear.com/deliberate-practice-theory — Magnus Carlsen accelerated chunk recognition (~300,000 configurations for elite chess players) by playing multiple simultaneous games; genes set limits, structured practice bridges raw ability to performance.
**Skill:** Write a 3-sentence deliberate practice plan for one skill you currently practice — identify the specific sub-component you'll isolate, the feedback mechanism you'll use, and the volume target.
**Reference doc:** `reference/deliberate-practice.html`

### Implementation
1. Pick one skill you want to improve (e.g., writing, coding, analysis).
2. Break it into sub-components. Write them down as a list — e.g., for writing: "sentence clarity," "argument structure," "opening hooks."
3. Pick the weakest sub-component. This is your practice target.
4. Design a minimum session: 20–30 minutes focused only on that sub-component.
5. Choose one feedback mechanism before you start — e.g., share with a peer, record yourself, compare against a benchmark example.
6. Set a volume target — e.g., 3 sessions this week.
7. After each session, write one sentence: what improved, what still felt awkward.
8. After one week, check: are you getting harder feedback, or just repeating the same comfortable motions? If the latter, raise the difficulty.

### Lesson 2: The Automaticity Trap — Why Most Practice Fails
**File:** `lessons/0002-automaticity-trap.html`
**Key concepts:** automaticity trap · feedback mechanisms · pattern-updating vs. pattern-reinforcing
**Source paper:** https://jamesclear.com/beginners-guide-deliberate-practice — The brain converts repeated behaviors into habits (automaticity), which reinforces existing patterns rather than creating new ones; measurement and external coaching are the two primary feedback mechanisms separating effective from ineffective practice.
**Skill:** Audit one habit or skill from your life: write down whether your current practice is pattern-updating (you get harder feedback, adjust behavior) or pattern-reinforcing (comfortable repetition). Name the missing feedback mechanism.
**Reference doc:** `reference/deliberate-practice.html` *(extend existing)*

### Implementation
1. List 3 skills or habits you practice regularly.
2. For each, ask: "Has this gotten meaningfully harder in the last 3 months?" If no, it has gone automatic.
3. For each automatic skill, write down the last time you received external feedback on it. If you can't remember, that's the problem.
4. Pick the most important automatic skill. Add one measurement to it — a score, a time, a count — something that makes progress or stagnation visible.
5. Find one external feedback source: a peer review, a recording of yourself, a checklist you check against after each session.
6. Run one session this week with that feedback mechanism active. Write one sentence after: did the feedback reveal something you hadn't noticed?
7. If the feedback revealed nothing new, raise the difficulty of the task before next session.

### Lesson 3: Spaced Repetition as a Pattern-Burning System
**File:** `lessons/0003-spaced-repetition-pattern-burning.html`
**Key concepts:** testing effect · spacing effect · conceptual SRS · attention programming
**Source paper:** https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Spaced_repetition_memory_system — SRS combines testing + spacing effects; beyond rote facts, it can develop conceptual understanding, "program attention" toward ideas, and train structural recognition rather than surface recall.
**Skill:** Write three SRS cards for a concept from this module — one testing recall of the key term, one testing understanding of the mechanism, and one requiring you to apply it to a novel scenario you invent.
**Reference doc:** `reference/spaced-repetition.html`

### Implementation
1. Install Anki (free, cross-platform) or enable the Spaced Repetition plugin in Obsidian.
2. After reading any article or completing any lesson, immediately write 3 cards before closing the material:
   - Card A (recall): "What is [key term]?" → one-sentence answer.
   - Card B (mechanism): "How does [key term] work?" → 2–3 sentence explanation.
   - Card C (application): Describe a scenario you invented where this concept applies → your answer.
3. Keep cards short — if an answer is longer than 3 sentences, split it into two cards.
4. Do your review queue every morning before starting other work — aim for under 10 minutes per day.
5. When you fail a card (can't recall), don't just re-read the answer. Write a new card that addresses the specific gap you just revealed.
6. Once a month, delete cards you've answered correctly 10+ times in a row — they're fully burned in.

## Module 2 — Mental Frameworks as Pattern Infrastructure
*Translates cognitive science into actionable tools: mental models are pre-loaded pattern templates that dramatically accelerate recognition speed. This module builds the model library itself.*

### Lesson 4: Mental Models as Pre-Loaded Pattern Templates
**File:** `lessons/0004-mental-models-pattern-templates.html`
**Key concepts:** mental model · cross-domain pattern transfer · first/second-order thinking · probabilistic thinking
**Source paper:** https://fs.blog/mental-models/ — Mental models compress complexity into manageable chunks; drawing from multiple disciplines (physics, biology, economics, mathematics) lets thinkers "jump jurisdictional boundaries" and spot patterns others miss.
**Skill:** Pick a recent decision or observation from your life. Apply three models from different disciplines (e.g., supply and demand, entropy, game theory) and write one insight each model surfaces that the others miss.
**Reference doc:** `reference/mental-models.html`

### Implementation
1. Pick a problem or decision you're currently facing. Write it in one sentence at the top of a blank page.
2. Choose three mental models from different disciplines. If you don't have a list yet, start with: supply and demand (economics), entropy (physics), natural selection (biology).
3. For each model, write: "If I look at this problem through the lens of [model], what do I see?" Write at least 2 sentences per model.
4. After all three, write: "What did model X reveal that models Y and Z missed?" — one sentence per model.
5. Circle the single most useful insight across all three. Write one action you'll take based on it.
6. Save the three models you used in a note called `mental-model-log.md` with the date and problem context — this becomes your evidence base for which models you actually use vs. just know about.

### Lesson 5: Building Your Model Library — The Munger Standard
**File:** `lessons/0005-munger-model-library.html`
**Key concepts:** model library · 80-90 model target · worldly wisdom · utility over completeness
**Source paper:** https://jamesclear.com/mental-models — Charlie Munger: "80 or 90 important models will carry about 90% of the freight in making you a worldly-wise person." No single model is perfect; broad library dramatically improves reasoning quality across virtually any situation.
**Skill:** Start your personal model inventory. List 10 mental models you already use (name + one-line description + one domain where you've applied it). Note gaps — domains where you have no model at all.
**Reference doc:** `reference/mental-models.html` *(extend existing)*

### Implementation
1. Create a note in Obsidian called `mental-models-library.md`.
2. Add a table with three columns: `Model Name` | `One-line description` | `Domain`.
3. Fill in 10 models you already know and have used — don't add models you've only heard of. Honesty here matters.
4. Below the table, list the major disciplines: physics, biology, chemistry, engineering, economics, psychology, mathematics, history, philosophy. For each, check whether you have at least one model from it.
5. Mark the disciplines with no model as gaps.
6. Pick one gap discipline. Spend 15 minutes reading its most foundational models (search "[discipline] mental models"). Add 2 new models to your table.
7. Repeat step 6 once per week until you have coverage across all disciplines.
8. Review the table monthly — remove models you've never actually applied, add ones you've started using.

### Lesson 6: First Principles Thinking — Deriving Patterns Yourself
**File:** `lessons/0006-first-principles-thinking.html`
**Key concepts:** first principles · Socratic questioning · Five Whys · analogical reasoning vs. first principles
**Source paper:** https://fs.blog/first-principles/ — First principles thinking strips problems to irreducible truths and rebuilds understanding from that foundation; contrasts with analogical reasoning (staying "in the shadow of others"). Rewards are non-linear because most people stop at borrowed patterns.
**Skill:** Choose a common belief or best-practice in your field. Apply the Five Whys until you hit bedrock. Write the irreducible truth you find and one new implication that the original belief obscured.
**Reference doc:** `reference/first-principles.html`

### Implementation
1. Write down one belief, best practice, or assumption you hold in your field — something you've accepted without deeply questioning.
2. Ask "Why is this true?" Write the answer in one sentence.
3. Ask "Why is *that* true?" Write the answer. Repeat until you can no longer answer — this is bedrock (typically 4–6 levels deep).
4. At bedrock, write: "The irreducible truth here is: ___." This is your first principle.
5. Now rebuild upward: given only this first principle, what would you design or conclude? Write 2–3 sentences.
6. Compare your rebuilt conclusion to the original belief. Write one thing the original belief got right and one thing it obscured or got wrong.
7. If the new implication is actionable, add it to your task list or project notes immediately.

### Lesson 7: Second-Order Thinking — Temporal Pattern Recognition
**File:** `lessons/0007-second-order-thinking.html`
**Key concepts:** second-order consequences · 10/10/10 temporal lenses · first-order-negative second-order-positive · model calibration
**Source paper:** https://fs.blog/second-order-thinking/ — Second-order thinking pushes beyond immediate solutions to downstream consequences ("And then what?"); many valuable opportunities are "first-order negative, second-order positive" because most people stop at surface-level analysis, reducing competition for those who think further.
**Skill:** Take a decision you made in the past 6 months. Map the 10-minute, 10-month, and 10-year consequences. Identify one second-order effect that you did not anticipate at the time.
**Reference doc:** `reference/second-order-thinking.html`

### Implementation
1. Write down a decision you're currently weighing, or one you recently made.
2. Draw a simple table with three columns: `10 minutes` | `10 months` | `10 years`.
3. In the 10-minute column: write the immediate, obvious consequence. This is the first-order effect — most people stop here.
4. In the 10-month column: ask "If this first-order effect plays out, what happens next?" Write 2–3 downstream consequences.
5. In the 10-year column: ask the same question again about your 10-month consequences. Write 1–2 long-horizon effects.
6. Look for a "first-order negative, second-order positive" pattern: something that looks bad immediately but good over time. Circle it if you find one.
7. Write one sentence: "The consequence I would have missed without this exercise is: ___."
8. File the table in your vault under the relevant project or area note.

## Module 3 — Vault Architecture for Pattern Surfacing
*Applies the cognitive principles to concrete vault design: atomic notes, dense links, and topology-first organization create the external structure that mirrors expert associative memory.*

### Lesson 8: Zettelkasten — Connection Over Collection
**File:** `lessons/0008-zettelkasten-connection-first.html`
**Key concepts:** atomicity principle · link context · structure notes · semilattice vs. tree
**Source paper:** https://zettelkasten.de/introduction/ — A Zettelkasten prioritizes connection over collection; when linking notes you must state *why* — that link context is itself new knowledge; structure notes create a dynamic semilattice rather than a rigid tree.
**Skill:** Take three notes you already have. For each pair (A→B, B→C, A→C), write one sentence of link context explaining *why* those two ideas connect. Count how many of those sentences contain a genuinely new insight.
**Reference doc:** `reference/zettelkasten.html`

### Implementation
1. Open Obsidian. Create a new note. Give it a title that is a single concept or claim — not a topic ("Second-order thinking amplifies edge"), not a source ("Notes on Farnam Street").
2. Write the note in 3–5 sentences maximum. One idea only.
3. Before closing the note, add at least one `[[link]]` to an existing note. Immediately after the link, write one sentence starting with "because" or "which means" — this is the link context.
4. If you can't write link context, the connection isn't strong enough — find a better one or leave it unlinked.
5. Once you have 10+ atomic notes on related ideas, create a **structure note**: a note whose only job is to list and sequence those atomic notes with brief commentary. Title it with a question or theme, not a topic.
6. Revisit structure notes monthly — add new atomic notes that belong, remove ones that no longer fit.
7. Never use folders to organize atomic notes. Let links and structure notes do the organizing.

### Lesson 9: Evergreen Notes — The Associative Network
**File:** `lessons/0009-evergreen-notes-associative-network.html`
**Key concepts:** atomic notes · concept-oriented (not source-oriented) · dense linking · associative over hierarchical
**Source paper:** https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Evergreen_notes — Five principles: atomic, concept-oriented, densely linked, associative over hierarchical, written for yourself; a well-linked system offloads pattern-location burden to the vault, allowing patterns to surface through navigation rather than recall.
**Skill:** Rewrite one of your own source-oriented notes ("Notes on book X") as a concept-oriented evergreen note (titled with an assertion, written for future-you, linked to 2+ existing notes). Compare the two versions and describe what changed.
**Reference doc:** `reference/evergreen-notes.html`

### Implementation
1. Open any existing note that starts with "Notes on…" or "Summary of…" — this is a source-oriented note.
2. Read through it and identify the single most important claim or insight it contains.
3. Create a new note. Title it with that claim as a declarative sentence — e.g., "Spaced repetition trains structural recognition, not surface recall."
4. Write the note in your own words, as if explaining it to yourself 6 months from now. No quotes, no attribution in the body.
5. Add a `Source:` line at the bottom pointing to the original note.
6. Add at least 2 `[[links]]` to other existing notes, each followed by one sentence of link context.
7. Delete or archive the source-oriented note — the evergreen note replaces it.
8. Repeat this process whenever you finish reading anything: extract 1–3 evergreen notes before closing the source.

### Lesson 10: Digital Gardens — Topology as Organizational Principle
**File:** `lessons/0010-digital-gardens-topology.html`
**Key concepts:** digital garden · topology vs. timeline · contextual relationships · knowledge maturation
**Source paper:** https://maggieappleton.com/garden-history — Digital gardens organize around contextual relationships rather than chronological order; information presented as topology (spatial arrangement) lets knowledge mature and cross-pollinate; clusters of related ideas become visible in ways impossible with date-sorted content.
**Skill:** Sketch a topology map of 8-10 concepts from your vault on paper (no software). Draw edges between concepts and label each edge with a relationship type (e.g., "causes," "enables," "contrasts with"). Identify the most connected node — is it the one you expected?
**Reference doc:** `reference/digital-gardens.html`

### Implementation
1. Open Obsidian Canvas (built-in) or use paper.
2. Drag or write 8–10 notes you've worked with recently onto the canvas.
3. Draw a line between any two that are related. Label each line with a relationship type: "causes," "enables," "contrasts with," "is a type of," "supports," or "contradicts."
4. Count the edges per node. The node with the most connections is your current thinking hub.
5. Ask: "Is this the concept I expected to be central?" If not, explore why — it may represent an under-acknowledged pattern.
6. Look for any two nodes with no path between them. If they seem like they should connect, write a new note that bridges them.
7. Save or photograph the map. Redo it monthly — the topology should shift as your understanding deepens. If it doesn't change at all, you're not adding genuinely new knowledge.

### Lesson 11: Obsidian Tooling — Native Features and Smart Connections
**File:** `lessons/0011-obsidian-tooling-smart-connections.html`
**Key concepts:** Obsidian Canvas · Smart Connections plugin · Web Clipper · local-first philosophy
**Source paper:** https://community.obsidian.md/ and https://obsidian.md/blog/ — 904+ integration plugins, 571+ visualization plugins; Smart Connections provides semantic pattern discovery (graph + list view); Canvas uses open JSON for spatial arrangement; local-first Markdown is the durable foundation minimizing fragile external dependencies.
**Skill:** Install Smart Connections (or open Graph View if not available). Open a note and identify 3 semantically related notes the tool surfaces that you would not have found by manual browsing. Write one new link for each, with link context.
**Reference doc:** `reference/obsidian-tooling.html`

### Implementation
1. **Install Smart Connections:** Open Obsidian → Settings → Community Plugins → Browse → search "Smart Connections" → Install → Enable.
2. Open any note in your vault. In the right sidebar, open the Smart Connections panel — it will show semantically related notes ranked by similarity.
3. Read the top 5 suggestions. For each, ask: "Would I have navigated here manually?" If not, it's a hidden connection.
4. For each hidden connection that is genuinely relevant, add a `[[link]]` with one sentence of link context in both notes.
5. **Use Graph View monthly:** Open Graph View (Ctrl/Cmd + G). Look for isolated nodes (notes with no links). Each isolated node is a missed connection opportunity.
6. **Set up Web Clipper:** Install the Obsidian Web Clipper browser extension. Configure it to save to your `raw/` folder with a date-prefix filename. Use it to clip any article worth keeping.
7. **Use Canvas for active projects:** Create one Canvas per active project. Link the notes you're working with spatially. Treat the canvas as a working surface, not an archive.

## Module 4 — Daily Workflow Integration
*Wires everything into a sustainable daily system: CODE for distillation, PARA for structure, and SRS for active pattern reinforcement. The daily loop is where patterns compound.*

### Lesson 12: CODE Framework — Distillation as Pattern Compression
**File:** `lessons/0012-code-framework-distillation.html`
**Key concepts:** CODE (Capture/Organize/Distill/Express) · Progressive Summarization · Intermediate Packets · cross-domain connection surfacing
**Source paper:** https://fortelabs.com/blog/basboverview/ — BASB's Distill step uses Progressive Summarization: layering highlights so notes can be reviewed at varying depths; as notes accumulate, cross-domain connections surface organically, acting as a mirror revealing underlying structures in thinking.
**Skill:** Take any note with more than 200 words. Apply Progressive Summarization: bold the most important 20%, then highlight the most important 20% of those bolded passages. Write a 2-sentence summary at the top. Does the summary reveal a pattern you hadn't noticed before?
**Reference doc:** `reference/code-para.html`

### Implementation
1. **Capture:** When you encounter useful information (article, conversation, observation), get it into your vault within 24 hours. Don't perfect it — just capture it.
2. **Organize:** File it under the relevant PARA category (Project, Area, Resource, or Archive). If you're unsure, use Resource.
3. **Distill — Pass 1:** Read the note once. Bold every sentence or phrase that seems important. Aim for 20% of the total text.
4. **Distill — Pass 2:** Read only the bolded text. Highlight the most important 20% of what you bolded. These are the loadbearing ideas.
5. **Distill — Pass 3:** At the top of the note, write a 2-sentence summary using only what you highlighted. No new ideas — compress what's already there.
6. **Express:** Use the note. Write from it, reference it in a meeting, link to it from a project. A note that is never expressed is just storage.
7. Do not distill every note — only ones you return to more than once. One-time captures don't need Progressive Summarization.

### Lesson 13: PARA — Keeping PKM Action-Oriented
**File:** `lessons/0013-para-action-oriented-structure.html`
**Key concepts:** PARA (Projects/Areas/Resources/Archives) · actionability over subject · organizing-as-procrastination · cadence of victories
**Source paper:** https://fortelabs.com/blog/para/ — PARA organizes by actionability not subject; organizing by outcome makes progress visible and prevents the vault from becoming a passive archive; "a cadence of regular victories" sustains the system long-term.
**Skill:** Audit your current note organization. Pick 10 notes and classify each into P/A/R/A. Identify any "resource" notes that are actually blocking an active project — those represent a hidden context switch waiting to happen.
**Reference doc:** `reference/code-para.html` *(extend existing)*

### Implementation
1. Create four top-level folders in Obsidian: `Projects/`, `Areas/`, `Resources/`, `Archive/`.
2. Define each for your context before moving anything:
   - **Projects:** Has a deadline and a finish line. e.g., "Launch feature X by March."
   - **Areas:** Ongoing responsibility with no end date. e.g., "Health," "Finance," "Team management."
   - **Resources:** Topics you're interested in but not actively working on. e.g., "Mental models," "Go programming."
   - **Archive:** Completed projects, inactive areas, outdated resources.
3. Pick 10 existing notes. Classify each. Move them to the correct folder.
4. If you find a note you can't classify, ask: "Is there a project or area this note serves?" If yes, it belongs there. If no, it's a Resource.
5. Each week, move completed projects to Archive immediately. This creates the "cadence of victories."
6. Do not create subfolders within Resources by topic — that is organizing-as-procrastination. Use tags or links instead.
7. Review your Projects folder weekly. Any project with no activity in 2 weeks needs a decision: do it, defer it, or archive it.

### Lesson 14: Daily SRS Loop — From Passive Archive to Active Pattern Reinforcement
**File:** `lessons/0014-daily-srs-loop.html`
**Key concepts:** attention programming · quality SRS prompts · emotional connection to review · SRS as deliberate practice for the mind's pattern library
**Source paper:** https://notes.andymatuschak.org/Spaced_repetition_memory_system — SRS beyond facts: "programs attention" toward ideas, automates retention so PKM work focuses on higher-order connections; emotional connection to review sessions matters for long-term consistency — SRS should feel like intellectual exercise, not rote drilling.
**Skill:** Design a 10-card SRS deck for the single most important insight from this entire course. Write 3 recall cards, 3 comprehension cards, and 4 application cards (cards requiring you to apply the idea to a novel scenario). Explain in one sentence why you chose that particular insight.
**Reference doc:** `reference/spaced-repetition.html` *(extend existing)*

### Implementation
1. **Set your daily review window:** Pick a fixed 10-minute slot — morning works best, before checking messages. Put it in your calendar as a recurring block.
2. **Open your SRS app** (Anki or Obsidian Spaced Repetition plugin) and work through all due cards. Do not skip cards — skipping breaks the spacing algorithm.
3. **Grading:** Be honest. Mark a card wrong if you hesitated more than 5 seconds, even if you eventually got it right. The algorithm needs accurate signal.
4. **Card creation rule:** Add new cards only at the end of a review session, not during. This keeps reviews fast and focused.
5. **Write application cards for your hardest insights:** For any concept you keep failing, write a new card that asks you to apply it to a scenario from your own work — not a textbook example. Personal context dramatically improves retention.
6. **Monthly deck audit:** Delete cards you've answered correctly 15+ times. Add cards for ideas you've been encountering repeatedly in your work but don't yet have cards for.
7. **Track streak, not perfection:** Missing one day is fine. Missing three days means the backlog will feel punishing — resume immediately and mark overdue cards as "hard" rather than working through each at full depth.

## Suggested Teaching Order

Follow the module sequence: Foundations → Frameworks → Vault Design → Daily Integration. Each module builds on the previous — understanding *why* patterns form in the brain (Module 1) makes the model library exercises (Module 2) meaningful; vault architecture (Module 3) is best understood after you have concepts to put in it; and the daily loop (Module 4) only sticks once the vault structure is in place.
